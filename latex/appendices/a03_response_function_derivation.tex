\chapter{Dyson Series and Response Functions for the n-th Order Polarization}
\label{app:response_functions}

Appendix~\ref{app:2dfts_maxwell} establishes the \emph{field-side} (Maxwell) connection between the measured complex signal field and the phase-matched nonlinear polarization. The purpose of this appendix is to provide the complementary \emph{matter-side} derivation: an explicit, causal expression for the $n$-th order polarization $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}$ in terms of the incident fields, starting from the equation of motion for the density operator.

\section{Matter-side: Dyson series and response functions for P(n)}
\label{app:sec:dyson_response}

The Maxwell-side result derived in Appendix~\ref{app:2dfts_maxwell} shows that, after phase matching, the radiated field is (to excellent approximation) a \emph{linear functional} of the nonlinear polarization (see in particular Eq.~\eqref{eq:signal_field_phase_matched} or the frequency-domain summary Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_Es_iomegaP}). To complete the Maxwell-to-signal connection, an explicit expression for $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}$ in terms of the incident fields is therefore needed. This section summarizes the standard response-function formalism used in nonlinear spectroscopy. It is the bridge between the microscopic model (Hamiltonian and dissipator encoded in $\mathcal{K}$) and the macroscopic source term that appears in the Maxwell equation.

\subsection{Equation of motion and interaction picture}

It is assumed that the reduced density operator $\hat{\rho}(t)$ of the material system obeys an equation of motion of the form
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
= -\mathrm{i}\,\mathcal{K}[\hat{\rho}(t)]
\quad + \mathrm{i}\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}(t)]\,E(t),
\label{eq:app_master_equation_with_field}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K}$ generates the field-free dynamics (Hamiltonian evolution and, if desired, a dissipator), $E(t)$ is the scalar field envelope along a chosen polarization direction, and
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}[\hat{A}] \equiv \frac{1}{\hbar}\,[\hat{\mu},\hat{A}],
\qquad \hat{\mu} \equiv \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\mathbf e,
\label{eq:app_dipole_superoperator}
\end{equation}
is the dipole superoperator (commutator form). The field-free propagator is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{U}_0(t) \equiv \exp(-\mathrm{i}\,\mathcal{K}t),
\label{eq:app_U0_def}
\end{equation}
meaning that in the absence of the field one has $\hat{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{U}_0(t-t_0)[\hat{\rho}(t_0)]$.

\subsection{Structure of the dipole superoperator}
\label{app:sec:dipole_superoperator_structure}

Before turning to perturbation theory, it is useful to make the algebraic structure of
$\mathcal{V}$ explicit. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_dipole_superoperator} one has
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}[\hat{A}] = \frac{1}{\hbar}\,[\hat{\mu},\hat{A}]
= \frac{1}{\hbar}\bigl(\hat{\mu}\,\hat{A} - \hat{A}\,\hat{\mu}\bigr),
\label{eq:app_V_commutator_expanded}
\end{equation}
so the field interaction consists of two pieces: a \emph{left action} of the dipole operator on
the density operator and a \emph{right action}. This ``left minus right'' commutator structure is the algebraic origin of the different Liouville-space (double-sided) pathways
encountered in nonlinear optics: whether the dipole acts on the ``ket'' (left) or on the ``bra'' (right)
determines which coherences or populations are created at each interaction (see also the main-text definition of the dipole superoperator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:dipole_superoperator_thesis}).

As in standard time-dependent perturbation theory, it is convenient to remove the field-free part by introducing the interaction-picture density operator
\begin{equation}
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t) \equiv \mathcal{U}_0(-t)[\hat{\rho}(t)],
\label{eq:app_rhoI_def}
\end{equation}
and the interaction-picture dipole superoperator
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm I}(t)
\equiv \mathcal{U}_0(-t)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(t).
\label{eq:app_VI_def}
\end{equation}
In this picture the equation of motion becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
= \mathrm{i}\,\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm I}(t)[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t)]\,E(t).
\label{eq:app_rhoI_eom}
\end{equation}
This is exact: the field-free dynamics has been removed, and all remaining time dependence is generated by the light--matter coupling.

\subsection{Integral equation and Dyson series}
\label{app:sec:dyson_series}

Equation~\eqref{eq:app_rhoI_eom} is a first-order inhomogeneous operator equation. Integrating from $t_0$ to $t$ gives the exact integral form
\begin{equation}
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t)
= \hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t_0)
\quad + \mathrm{i}\int_{t_0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm I}(\tau)[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(\tau)]\,E(\tau).
\label{eq:app_rhoI_integral}
\end{equation}
Note that the initial condition is the same in both pictures: $\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t_0)=\hat{\rho}(t_0)$.

In direct analogy to wavefunction-based time-dependent perturbation theory, one may interpret the resulting expansion as an evolution of the density matrix in powers of the interaction-picture coupling: $\hat{\rho}^{(0)}(t)$ is the zeroth-order density matrix (field-free evolution), and the higher-order corrections $\hat{\rho}^{(n)}(t)$ are ordered in powers of the interaction Hamiltonian/superoperator. A key difference compared to the wavefunction is that the density operator carries both a ket and a bra; consequently, the light--matter interaction can act from the left or from the right. This left/right (ket/bra) structure is precisely what later appears as the commutator (``left minus right'') form of the dipole superoperator and underlies the Liouville-space pathway picture used in nonlinear spectroscopy~\cite{hammzanni2011conceptsmethods2d}.

To obtain a perturbation expansion in the field, this equation is iterated: the right-hand side is substituted back into $\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(\tau)$ repeatedly. After $n$ iterations one arrives at the Dyson series
\begin{equation}
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t)
= \Biggl[
\mathds{1}
+ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (\mathrm{i})^n
\int_{t_0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1
\int_{t_0}^{\tau_1}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_2\,\cdots
\int_{t_0}^{\tau_{n-1}}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_n\,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm I}(\tau_1)\cdots\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm I}(\tau_n)
E(\tau_1)\cdots E(\tau_n)
\Biggr][\hat{\rho}(t_0)].
\label{eq:app_dyson}
\end{equation}
The nested integration limits enforce the time ordering $\tau_1>\tau_2>\cdots>\tau_n$ of the interaction times.

Formally, one writes this as a time-ordered exponential,
\begin{equation}
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t)
= \mathcal{T}\exp\!\left[\,\mathrm{i}\int_{t_0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm I}(\tau)\,E(\tau)\right] \hat{\rho}(t_0),
\label{eq:app_time_ordered_exponential}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ is the time-ordering operator. This notation is compact but symbolic; for spectroscopy the explicit $n$-th order terms are used.

\subsection{Polarization as a perturbation series}
\label{app:sec:polarization_series}

The macroscopic polarization is the expectation value of the dipole operator (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:P_def_spectroscopy}).
Using $\hat{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{U}_0(t)\,[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t)]$ and cyclicity of the trace, this can be written in interaction-picture form as
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{P}(t)
= \mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\mathrm I}(t)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}(t)\}
= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t),
\label{eq:app_P_perturbation_series}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\mathrm I}(t)$ is the interaction-picture dipole operator corresponding to the field-free dynamics (equivalently, the Heisenberg-evolved dipole under $\mathcal{K}$). In Liouville-space notation one may view this as applying the adjoint propagator to the observable, $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\mathrm I}(t)=\mathcal{U}_0^{\dagger}(t)[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}]$.
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t) = \mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\mathrm I}(t)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm I}^{(n)}(t)\}
\label{eq:app_Pn_def}
\end{equation}
the contribution containing $n$ factors of the field.

It is often useful to rewrite $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}$ directly in terms of the field-free propagators $\mathcal{U}_0$ and the Schr\"odinger-picture superoperator $\mathcal{V}$. Keeping the time ordering explicit, one obtains an expression of the schematic form
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t)
&= (\mathrm{i})^n
\int_{t_0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}\tau^{(1)}\cdots
\int_{t_0}^{\tau^{(n-1)}}\!\mathrm{d}\tau^{(n)}\,
\\
&\quad \mathrm{Tr}\Bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\mathcal{U}_0(t-\tau^{(1)})\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau^{(1)}-\tau^{(2)})\,\mathcal{V}\cdots\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau^{(n)}-t_0)[\hat{\rho}(t_0)]\Bigr\}
\prod_{j=1}^{n} E(\tau^{(j)}),
\end{split}
\label{eq:app_Pn_explicit}
\end{equation}
Reading from right to left inside the trace makes the physics transparent: starting from the initial state, the system undergoes alternating sequences of field interactions ($\mathcal{V}$) and free evolution ($\mathcal{U}_0$), followed by the dipole readout.

\subsection{Relative delays and the causal response function}
\label{app:sec:relative_delays}

For spectroscopy it is natural to re-parameterize the ordered interaction times $\tau^{(1)}>\cdots>\tau^{(n)}$ by \emph{relative delays} between successive events.
The idea is to replace the absolute times by the durations of the free-evolution intervals between interactions.
Define
\begin{equation}
\tau_n = t-\tau^{(1)},\qquad
\tau_{n-j}=\tau^{(j)}-\tau^{(j+1)}\ (j=1,\ldots,n-1),
\label{eq:app_relative_delay_definition}
\end{equation}
so that $\tau_j\ge 0$ and, explicitly,
\begin{equation}
\tau^{(n)} = t-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tau_k,\qquad
\tau^{(n-1)} = t-\sum_{k=2}^{n}\tau_k,\qquad \ldots,\qquad
\tau^{(1)} = t-\tau_n.
\label{eq:app_change_of_variables}
\end{equation}
The Jacobian of this transformation is unity.
Moreover, if the initial time is sent to the remote past ($t_0\to-\infty$), then the nested integration region
$t_0<\tau^{(n)}<\cdots<\tau^{(1)}<t$ maps to independent semi-infinite integrals $t_j\in[0,\infty)$.

\medskip
\noindent
Moreover, if the initial time is sent to the remote past ($t_0\to-\infty$), then the nested integration region
$t_0<\tau^{(n)}<\cdots<\tau^{(1)}<t$ maps to independent semi-infinite integrals $\tau_j\in[0,\infty)$.

\medskip
\noindent
This change of variables is commonly depicted by distinguishing \emph{absolute} interaction times from the experimentally controlled \emph{time intervals} between interactions~\cite{hammzanni2011conceptsmethods2d}. In the present appendix, $\tau^{(j)}$ is used for the absolute, time-ordered interaction times and $\tau_j$ is used for the nonnegative delay intervals.

If the system is in equilibrium for all earlier times, $\hat{\rho}(t_0)=\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}$, then the initial propagation becomes trivial in the same limit,
since $\mathcal{U}_0(t)[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]=\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}$ for all $t$.
In that case the $n$-th order polarization takes the standard causal convolution form
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t)
= \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_n\cdots\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1\,
\boldsymbol{S}^{(n)}(\tau_n,\ldots,\tau_1)
E(t-\tau_n)\,E(t-\tau_n-\tau_{n-1})\cdots E\Bigl(t-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tau_k\Bigr),
\label{eq:app_Pn_convolution}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{response function} is defined by
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{S}^{(n)}(\tau_n,\ldots,\tau_1)
\equiv (\mathrm{i})^n\,\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_n)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_{n-1})\,\mathcal{V}\cdots\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\Bigr\}.
\label{eq:app_Sn_def}
\end{equation}

\medskip
\noindent
Equation~\eqref{eq:app_Sn_def} makes explicit what is meant by a (causal) \emph{response function}: $\boldsymbol{S}^{(n)}$ describes the intrinsic material response to $n$ light--matter interactions and depends only on the field-free dynamics (through $\mathcal{U}_0$) and the dipole operator (through $\mathcal{V}$), not on the particular pulse shapes~\cite{hamm2005principlesnonlinearoptical}. The convolution form additionally assumes that the system is prepared in an equilibrium state $\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}$ for $t\to-\infty$ and that the field interactions can be treated as localized in time (short-pulse/impulsive limit).

\noindent
The nested-commutator structure is crucial: since $\mathcal{V}[\hat{A}]=\hbar^{-1}[\hat{\mu},\hat{A}]$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_dipole_superoperator}), each interaction produces a \emph{left} and a \emph{right} action on the density operator (Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_V_commutator_expanded}). As a result, expanding the product of $n$ commutators generates $2^n$ Liouville-space contributions (``pathways'') before additional physical selections (e.g. rotating-wave approximation, phase matching/phase cycling) restrict the subset that contributes to a given measured signal. These pathways are naturally represented by double-sided Feynman (Liouville-space) diagrams, where the two branches represent the ket and bra evolution and each field interaction is drawn as an arrow acting from the left or from the right~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}.

\noindent
Finally, while the Dyson series itself is time ordered (Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_dyson}), in an experiment different pulse arrival orders correspond to different time-ordered contributions to $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}$; the total polarization is obtained by summing the relevant orderings for the chosen pulse sequence.

Two points are worth emphasizing. First, causality is explicit: $\boldsymbol{S}^{(n)}$ is only needed for nonnegative delays, and the fields are evaluated at times earlier than $t$. Second, $\boldsymbol{S}^{(n)}$ depends only on field-free dynamics and the dipole operator; it is independent of the particular pulse shapes.

\subsection{Tensorial structure (optional but useful)}
\label{app:sec:tensorial_structure}

If one does not fix a single polarization direction, the response becomes a rank-$(n{+}1)$ tensor. Introduce component dipole superoperators
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}_\beta[\hat{A}] \equiv \frac{1}{\hbar}[\hat{\mu}_\beta,\hat{A}],\qquad \beta\in\{x,y,z\},
\label{eq:app_Vbeta_def}
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
S^{(n)}_{\alpha\beta_1\cdots\beta_n}(\tau_n,\ldots,\tau_1)
= (\mathrm{i})^n\,\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl\{\hat{\mu}_\alpha\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_n)\,\mathcal{V}_{\beta_n}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_{n-1})\,\mathcal{V}_{\beta_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}_{\beta_1}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\Bigr\}.
\label{eq:app_Sn_tensor_def}
\end{equation}
Then $P^{(n)}_\alpha(t)$ is obtained by contracting this tensor with the field components $E_{\beta}(t)$. Choosing a fixed unit polarization $\mathbf e$ and writing $E_\beta(t)=e_\beta E(t)$ reduces the tensorial form back to Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_Pn_convolution}.



\subsection{Linear and third-order response (\texorpdfstring{$n=1$ and $n=3$}{n=1 and n=3})}
\label{app:sec:linear_and_third_order_response}

Since the central experimental observable in this thesis is a third-order signal, it is useful to write the lowest- and third-order cases explicitly.

\paragraph{Linear response.}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_Pn_convolution},
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}(t)
= \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1\,\boldsymbol{S}^{(1)}(\tau_1)\,E(t-\tau_1),
\label{eq:app_P1_convolution}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{S}^{(1)}(\tau_1)
= \mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\bigr\}.
\label{eq:app_S1_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Third-order response.}
Likewise,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(t)
= \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_3\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_2\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1\,\boldsymbol{S}^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
\,E(t-\tau_3)\,E(t-\tau_3-\tau_2)\,E(t-\tau_3-\tau_2-\tau_1),
\label{eq:app_P3_convolution}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{S}^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
= (\mathrm{i})^3\,\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_3)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_2)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\Bigr\}.
\label{eq:app_S3_def}
\end{equation}
In a multi-pulse experiment one typically decomposes $E(t)$ into pulse envelopes (e.g. $E_1+E_2+E_3$) and selects the desired third-order term by phase matching or phase cycling; Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_P3_convolution} is the causal kernel underlying that selection~\cite{hammzanni2011conceptsmethods2d}.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Frequency-domain bookkeeping (optional).}
In the frequency domain (after splitting each field into positive/negative-frequency parts), a compact bookkeeping form for third-order mixing can be written as
\begin{equation}
P_i^{(3)}(t)
=
\varepsilon_0
\sum_{jkl}
\sum_{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3=\pm 1}
\chi^{(3)}_{ijkl}\big(-\omega_S;\sigma_1\omega_1,\sigma_2\omega_2,\sigma_3\omega_3\big)
\,E_{1,j}^{(\sigma_1)}E_{2,k}^{(\sigma_2)}E_{3,l}^{(\sigma_3)}\,e^{-i\omega_S t},
\label{eq:app_P3_freq_domain_bookkeeping_tensor}
\end{equation}
with $\omega_S=\sigma_1\omega_1+\sigma_2\omega_2+\sigma_3\omega_3$. Fixing the polarization directions of all beams reduces \eqref{eq:app_P3_freq_domain_bookkeeping_tensor} to a scalar expression of the form used in the main text.


\paragraph{Remark.}
It is worth pausing to appreciate what has been achieved. The $n$-th order polarization $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t)$ is obtained by taking the trace of the dipole operator with the $n$-th order contribution to the density operator generated by the Dyson series. In nonlinear spectroscopy this is not merely a formal approximation scheme: each term of fixed order corresponds to a real, experimentally isolable physical process, and the response function $\boldsymbol{S}^{(n)}$ provides the system-specific, causal kernel that the incident fields probe.
