\chapter{Maxwell-to-Signal Derivation (Field Side)}
\label{app:2dfts_maxwell}

This appendix presents the \emph{field-side} derivation for coherent 2D-FTS/2DES, showing how Maxwell's equations lead to a heterodyne-detected signal field linear in the third-order polarization $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$. It justifies the proportionality used in \autoref{sec:pol_and_macr_response}.

The logic is: (i) Maxwell $\Rightarrow$ a driven wave equation with $\boldsymbol{P}$ as source; (ii) phase matching and SVEA $\Rightarrow$ the emitted field in a chosen direction is proportional to the corresponding component of $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$; (iii) the microscopic (matter-side) expression for $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ is derived in \autoref{app:response_functions}.

\section{From Maxwell's equations to a wave equation for the radiation field}
\label{app:sec:maxwell_to_wave}

In this section it is made explicit (in the same spirit as the lecture notes)\todoimp{i have to actually cite the lecture} why the macroscopic polarization is the source of the emitted optical field. The key conceptual point is that only the \emph{transverse} (radiation) part of the electromagnetic field propagates as a wave, and it is sourced by the transverse current. In condensed-matter optics, that current is conveniently written in terms of the polarization.

\subsection{Maxwell's equations and electromagnetic potentials}

The fundamental laws are introduced first. In SI units, Maxwell's equations in the presence of charge and current densities $\varrho(\boldsymbol{r},t)$ and $\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{r},t)$ read
\begin{align}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla} \times \boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	 & = \mu_0\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{r},t) + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t},
	\label{eq:app_maxwell_ampere}
	\\
	\boldsymbol{\nabla} \times \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	 & = -\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t},
	\label{eq:app_maxwell_faraday}
	\\
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	 & = \frac{\varrho(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\varepsilon_0},
	\label{eq:app_maxwell_gaussE}
	\\
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot \boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	 & = 0.
	\label{eq:app_maxwell_gaussB}
\end{align}
Here $\boldsymbol{E}$ is the electric field, $\boldsymbol{B}$ the magnetic field and the constants satisfy $c^2 = 1/(\mu_0\varepsilon_0)$.

It is often convenient to express $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ in terms of a
vector potential $\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)$ and a scalar potential
$\phi(\mathbf r,t)$ by
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{B} = \boldsymbol{\nabla}\times \boldsymbol{A},
	\qquad
	\boldsymbol{E} = -\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{A}}{\partial t} - \boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi.
	\label{eq:app_potentials_def}
\end{equation}
This reformulation automatically satisfies Faraday's law \cref{eq:app_maxwell_faraday} and the magnetic Gauss law \cref{eq:app_maxwell_gaussB}, eliminating two equations and simplifying the system.

\subsection{Gauge freedom and Coulomb gauge}

To proceed from the potential formulation, a gauge must be chosen. While the potentials reformulation of \autoref{eq:app_potentials_def} is useful, the potentials are not unique: for any scalar function $\chi(\boldsymbol{r},t)$,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{A}' = \boldsymbol{A} + \boldsymbol{\nabla}\chi,
	\qquad
	\phi' = \phi - \frac{\partial \chi}{\partial t},
\end{equation}
leaves $\boldsymbol{E}$ and $\boldsymbol{B}$ invariant. To obtain a unique solution this gauge freedom must be fixed. For optics, the Coulomb gauge is chosen
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A} = 0.
\end{equation*}
With this gauge choice, inserting the potential definitions \autoref{eq:app_potentials_def} into electric Gauss' law \autoref{eq:app_maxwell_gaussE} gives the Poisson equation
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\phi(\boldsymbol{r},t) = -\frac{\varrho(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\varepsilon_0},
\end{equation}
which determines $\phi$ instantaneously in space. This solution does \emph{not} violate causality---it is a gauge artifact that describes the instantaneous Coulomb interaction. The causal, propagating part of the optical field is contained in the transverse (vector potential) components discussed next.

\subsection{Transverse/longitudinal decomposition}

To separate the propagating (radiative) part of the field from the instantaneous Coulomb part, the fields are decomposed into transverse and longitudinal components. Only the transverse component carries radiative degrees of freedom.

Formally, any sufficiently well-behaved vector field $\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{r})$ admits the Helmholtz decomposition into irrotational and solenoidal parts:
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{F} = \boldsymbol{F}^{\perp} + \boldsymbol{F}^{\parallel},
\end{equation}
where the transverse part is divergence-free and the longitudinal part is curl-free
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}^{\perp}=0,\qquad \boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{F}^{\parallel}=0.
\end{equation}
Applying this decomposition to the electric field from \autoref{eq:app_potentials_def},
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E} = \boldsymbol{E}^{\perp} + \boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp} = -\partial_t \boldsymbol{A}$ (using Coulomb gauge) and $\boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel} = -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi$. Under this decomposition, Maxwell's equations \cref{eq:app_maxwell_ampere,eq:app_maxwell_faraday,eq:app_maxwell_gaussE,eq:app_maxwell_gaussB} split into independent transverse and longitudinal parts:
\begin{align}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}     & = -\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{B}}{\partial t},
	                                                    &
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel} & = 0,
	\\
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel}  & = \frac{\varrho}{\varepsilon_0},
	                                                    &
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}      & = 0.
\end{align}
Faraday's law only constrains the transverse field because $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel}=0$ identically, while Gauss' law fixes the longitudinal field through the charge density. Thus, the propagating and Coulomb degrees of freedom are fully separated.

\medskip

To understand which current sources what, recall that charge and current are not independent: they are linked by the continuity equation
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \varrho(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{r},t)=0.
	\label{eq:app_continuity}
\end{equation}
Decomposing $\boldsymbol{j}=\boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}+\boldsymbol{j}^{\parallel}$ and using $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}=0$ shows that \autoref{eq:app_continuity} constrains only the longitudinal part:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \varrho}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}^{\parallel}=0.
\end{equation}
This is the precise sense in which $\boldsymbol{j}^{\parallel}$ is tied to instantaneous Coulomb (near-field) physics, while radiative fields are sourced by $\boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}$.

Two immediate consequences are noted:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Because $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}=0$, the magnetic field is purely transverse: $\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}^{\perp}$.
	\item In Coulomb gauge $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}=0$, the vector potential is purely transverse: $\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\perp}$.
\end{itemize}
Combining the decomposition with the Coulomb gauge choice and \autoref{eq:app_potentials_def}, one identifies
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp} = -\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{A}}{\partial t},
	\qquad
	\boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel} = -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi.
\end{equation}
Crucially, the radiation field relevant for propagating optics is $\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}$. The longitudinal field $\boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel}$ is determined instantaneously by $\varrho$ via the Poisson equation and does not represent a propagating degree of freedom.

\subsection{Wave equation for the transverse electric field}

With the transverse (radiative) field component isolated, the wave equation it satisfies is derived. Starting from Faraday's law \autoref{eq:app_maxwell_faraday}, the curl is taken on both sides:
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E})
	= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}\big).
\end{equation}
Next, eliminate $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}$ using Ampère--Maxwell's law \autoref{eq:app_maxwell_ampere} and use the relation $\mu_0\varepsilon_0=1/c^2$ to obtain
\begin{equation}
	-\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E})
	= \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\boldsymbol{E}}{\partial t^2} + \mu_0\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{j}}{\partial t}.
\end{equation}
Only transverse parts contribute to the curl--curl operator because $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\parallel}=0$. Likewise, the radiated field is sourced by the transverse current $\boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}$. Projection onto the transverse sector therefore gives
\begin{equation}
	-\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp})
	= \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}}{\partial t^2} + \mu_0\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}}{\partial t}.
\end{equation}
Using the identity $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}) = \boldsymbol{\nabla}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}) - \boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}$ and $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}=0$, one obtains the driven wave equation
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	- \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t^2}
	= \mu_0\,\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t}.
	\label{eq:app_wave_E_perp_j}
\end{equation}


\subsection{From transverse current to polarization}
The wave equation \autoref{eq:app_wave_E_perp_j} is expressed in terms of the current $\boldsymbol{j}^{\perp}$. In condensed-matter optics, it is more convenient to work with the macroscopic polarization $\boldsymbol{P}$. On large scales, the movement of bound charges in a neutral dielectric material is described by the polarization density $\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t)$. When averaged over many particles, the current due to these bound charges equals the time derivative of the polarization,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{r},t) = \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t}.
	\label{eq:app_j_equals_Pdot}
\end{equation}
Together with the continuity equation \autoref{eq:app_continuity}, this implies the standard relation between bound charge density and polarization,
\begin{equation}
	\varrho_b(\boldsymbol{r},t) = -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t).
\end{equation}
Substituting the polarization current \autoref{eq:app_j_equals_Pdot} into \autoref{eq:app_wave_E_perp_j} gives the wave equation in the practically useful polarization form:
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	- \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t^2}
	= \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t^2}.
	\label{eq:app_wave_with_P}
\end{equation}
This is a crucial result: \autoref{eq:app_wave_with_P} explicitly shows that the macroscopic polarization $\boldsymbol{P}$ is the source of the radiation field $\boldsymbol{E}^{\perp}$. This is the central Maxwell-side justification for spectroscopy: by measuring the optical field emitted from a material, one can extract information about the polarization induced by the incident light.

\medskip

To apply this result to nonlinear spectroscopy, the polarization is decomposed as $\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathrm{NL}}$, where $\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathrm{NL}}$ contains the higher-order contributions (e.g., $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ for third-order 2D-FTS). The linear response $\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}$ can be absorbed into an effective complex refractive index $n(\omega)$ defined by $n^2 = 1 + \chi^{(1)}$. With this redefinition, \autoref{eq:app_wave_with_P} becomes the nonlinear wave equation:
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\nabla}^2 \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	- \frac{n^2}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	= \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathrm{NL}}(\boldsymbol{r},t).
	\label{eq:app_wave_eq_nonlinear}
\end{equation}
In a three-pulse coherent 2D experiment operating in the weak-field perturbative regime, only the lowest nonlinear order contributes significantly to the signal. Therefore, the relevant nonlinear source is (to leading order)
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathrm{NL}}(\boldsymbol{r},t) \approx \boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ is cubic in the incident field. 

The Maxwell-side source term has now been obtained. Two remaining tasks are: (i) connecting this source to a measurable signal field via the slowly-varying envelope approximation and phase matching, and (ii) providing the microscopic matter-side expression for $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ in terms of the incident fields.

\section{Input fields, phase factors, and phase matching}
\label{app:sec:phase_matching}

Before proceeding to solve the wave equation \autoref{eq:app_wave_eq_nonlinear}, the form of the incident fields that drive the nonlinear response is specified. The incident field is represented as a sum of three pulses $j\in\{1,2,3\}$ with slowly varying envelopes. The carrier phases (phase kicks) $\phi_j$ are written explicitly for completeness. They are essential for phase cycling in collinear geometries (see \autoref{chapter_spectroscopy}, \autoref{sec:phase_cycling}).
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}_{\mathrm{in}}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	= \sum_{j=1}^{3}\Big[
	\boldsymbol{E}_j(t-t_j)\,e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_j\cdot\boldsymbol{r}-\omega_L t + \phi_j)} + \text{c.c.}
	\Big].
	\label{eq:app_incoming_field}
\end{equation}
In the perturbative regime, the third-order polarization $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ is cubic in the incident fields. When \autoref{eq:app_incoming_field} is substituted into the expression for $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ (derived on the matter side), many different terms emerge with different plane-wave phase factors. Under the rotating-wave approximation (which assumes equal carrier frequencies), only the contributions oscillating near $\omega_L$ are retained. These correspond to having exactly one complex-conjugated field and two non-conjugated fields in the polarization, producing the third-order signal wavevector combinations
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{k}_S = \pm\boldsymbol{k}_1 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_2 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_3,
\end{equation*}
and a corresponding signed carrier-phase combination $\Phi = \pm\phi_1\pm\phi_2\pm\phi_3$.

Macroscopic emission from a finite sample thickness builds up coherently only when the polarization wave and the radiated wave remain approximately phase matched. This is the origin of the standard separation into signal directions. The two key third-order directions for 2D electronic spectroscopy are
\begin{align*}
\boldsymbol{k}_{R}  & = -\boldsymbol{k}_1 + \boldsymbol{k}_2 + \boldsymbol{k}_3 \qquad \text{(rephasing / photon echo)},
\\
\boldsymbol{k}_{NR} & = +\boldsymbol{k}_1 - \boldsymbol{k}_2 + \boldsymbol{k}_3 \qquad \text{(nonrephasing / FID)}.
\end{align*}


\section{Slowly varying envelope approximation and the emitted field}
\label{app:sec:svea}

The driven wave equation \autoref{eq:app_wave_eq_nonlinear} is solved to connect the nonlinear polarization to the observed signal field. The key is the slowly-varying envelope approximation (SVEA), which exploits the fact that the field envelopes vary slowly compared to the optical oscillation. Under SVEA, the emitted field in the phase-matched direction is proportional to the corresponding component of the nonlinear polarization.

A slab sample of thickness $L$ along the $z$ direction is considered, and a single phase-matched signal component with carrier frequency $\omega_L$ and wavevector propagating along $z$ is selected. Starting from \autoref{eq:app_wave_eq_nonlinear}, the relevant Fourier component of the third-order polarization is extracted,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{P}_{\text{NL}}(\boldsymbol{r},t) \supset \boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)\,e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_S\cdot\boldsymbol{r}-\omega_L t)} + \text{c.c.}
	\label{eq:app_pol_component}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)$ is the slowly-varying envelope. A corresponding forward-propagating field solution is sought in terms of an envelope,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}_S(\boldsymbol{r},t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{E}_S(z,t)\,e^{i(k'_S z-\omega_L t)} + \text{c.c.},
	\label{eq:app_field_ansatz}
\end{equation}
that solves \autoref{eq:app_wave_eq_nonlinear} with dispersion relation $k'_S=n(\omega_L)\omega_L/c$ (the phase velocity in the medium).

\subsection{Slowly varying envelope approximation (SVEA)}
Before solving \autoref{eq:app_wave_eq_nonlinear}, a key timescale separation is used. The SVEA assumes envelopes $\boldsymbol{E}_S(z,t)$, $\boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)$ vary slowly compared to optical oscillations
\begin{equation}
	\left|\partial_t \boldsymbol{E}_S\right| \ll \omega_L\,\left|\boldsymbol{E}_S\right|,
	\qquad
	\left|\partial_z^2 \boldsymbol{E}_S\right| \ll k'_S\,\left|\partial_z \boldsymbol{E}_S\right|.
	\label{eq:app_svea_conditions}
\end{equation}
This separation of timescales allows higher-order envelope derivatives to be dropped when they appear with fast oscillatory factors.

\subsubsection{Spatial and temporal derivatives}
To extract the slowly-varying envelope behavior, only the positive-frequency part is kept and the explicit time dependence is suppressed for the moment. Consider a positive-frequency component
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}_S^{(+)}(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{E}_S(z)\,e^{i k'_S z}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{align}
	\partial_z \boldsymbol{E}_S^{(+)}
	 & = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\partial_z \boldsymbol{E}_S + i k'_S\boldsymbol{E}_S\right)e^{i k'_S z},
	\\
	\partial_z^2 \boldsymbol{E}_S^{(+)}
	 & = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\partial_z^2 \boldsymbol{E}_S + 2 i k'_S\partial_z \boldsymbol{E}_S - k_S'^2\boldsymbol{E}_S\right)e^{i k'_S z}
	\approx \tfrac{1}{2}\left(2 i k'_S\partial_z \boldsymbol{E}_S - k_S'^2\boldsymbol{E}_S\right)e^{i k'_S z},
	\label{eq:app_second_derivative_svea}
\end{align}
where the last step uses the SVEA condition \autoref{eq:app_svea_conditions} to drop $\partial_z^2\boldsymbol{E}_S$. % TODO i dont know if this is correct?: The key insight is that the first derivative is multiplied by the large wavevector $k'_S$, so even the small envelope variations produce significant contributions.

For the time dependence, the corresponding approximation is
\begin{equation}
	\partial_t^2\!\left[\boldsymbol{E}_S(z,t)e^{-i\omega_L t}\right] \approx -\omega_L^2\,\boldsymbol{E}_S(z,t)e^{-i\omega_L t},
	\qquad
	\partial_t^2\!\left[\boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)e^{-i\omega_L t}\right] \approx -\omega_L^2\,\boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)e^{-i\omega_L t}.
	\label{eq:app_time_derivatives_svea}
\end{equation}
Under SVEA, the envelopes vary slowly, so rapid oscillations at frequency $\omega_L$ dominate over envelope variations. Thus, higher time derivatives of the slowly-varying parts are negligible.

\subsection{Envelope propagation equation}
The ansätze \autoref{eq:app_pol_component} and \autoref{eq:app_field_ansatz} are substituted into the nonlinear wave equation \autoref{eq:app_wave_eq_nonlinear}. Using the SVEA approximations of the spatial and temporal derivatives (\autoref{eq:app_second_derivative_svea} and \autoref{eq:app_time_derivatives_svea}) and imposing the dispersion relation $k_S'^2=n^2(\omega_L)\omega_L^2/c^2$, the large ``homogeneous'' oscillatory terms cancel exactly. This leaves a first-order propagation equation for the slowly-varying envelope:
\begin{equation}
	i k'_S\,\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{E}_S(z,t)}{\partial z}
	= -\frac{\omega_L^2}{\varepsilon_0 c^2}\,\boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)\,e^{i\Delta k\,z},
	\label{eq:app_envelope_propagation}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta k = k_S - k'_S$ is the phase mismatch: the difference between the wavevector of the polarization wave and the free-space dispersion relation $k'_S = n(\omega_L)\omega_L/c$. This crucial first-order propagation equation shows how the field amplitude is built up layer by layer through the material by the induced polarization.

\subsection{Integration and sinc factor}
\autoref{eq:app_envelope_propagation} is integrated across the sample. Assuming no signal at the entrance, $\boldsymbol{E}_S(0,t)=0$ (the signal is generated only by the nonlinear polarization inside), integration from $z=0$ to $z=L$ yields the signal field exiting the sample:
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}_S(L,t)
	= i\,\frac{\omega_L}{n(\omega_L)\varepsilon_0 c}\,\boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)\,L\,e^{i\Delta k L/2}\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\Delta k L}{2}\right).
	\label{eq:app_sinc}
\end{equation}
This result shows that, up to sample-geometry factors and phase-matching effects (encoded in the $\mathrm{sinc}$ and exponential factors), the emitted signal field is directly proportional to the (phase-matched component of the) third-order polarization.

In a well-designed experiment, the phase-matching condition is satisfied: $\Delta k\approx 0$. In this limit, $\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta k L/2)\to 1$ and $e^{i\Delta k L/2}\to 1$. Therefore, from \autoref{eq:app_sinc}, the emitted (complex) signal field at the sample exit becomes
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}_S(t)\equiv \boldsymbol{E}_S(L,t)
	\propto i\,\frac{\omega_L}{n(\omega_L)\varepsilon_0 c}\,\boldsymbol{P}_S^{(3)}(t)\,L.
	\label{eq:signal_field_phase_matched}
\end{equation}
This is the key Maxwell-side result for 2DFT spectroscopy: in the phase-matched limit, the measured complex signal field is linear in (and shifted in phase by $\pi/2$ relative to) the corresponding component of $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$. Thus, measuring the emitted field directly provides information about the nonlinear polarization response of the material.

In the frequency domain, one often summarizes this proportionality in the compact form
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}_S(\omega) = \kappa\, i\omega\,\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(\omega),
	\label{eq:app_Es_iomegaP}
\end{equation}
with a real prefactor $\kappa$ that depends on propagation conventions and experimental geometry. \autoref{eq:app_Es_iomegaP} is the key Maxwell-side link: it demonstrates that the measured field is a linear functional of $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$, converting the nonlinear spectroscopy measurement into a linear inversion problem.

\medskip

The Maxwell-side (field) derivation is now complete. The connection is as follows: the incident fields drive the matter system (described on the matter side by the Dyson series and response functions from \autoref{app:response_functions}) to produce a third-order nonlinear polarization $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$. This polarization, in turn, sources the radiated optical field that is measured experimentally. What remains is the microscopic expression for $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ in terms of the incident fields, which is provided in \autoref{app:response_functions}.
