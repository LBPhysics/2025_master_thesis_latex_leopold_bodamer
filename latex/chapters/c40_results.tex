This chapter combines the open-system framework of~\autoref{chapter:oqs_open_quantum_systems} with the spectroscopy workflow of~\autoref{chapter:spctr_spectroscopy}. Its central aim is to reproduce selected spectra from Papers~I and II~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse,pisliakovetal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse} within one consistent QuTiP-based workflow. For both the monomer and dimer, the spectra were first reconstructed with the explicit paper equations (see~\autoref{app:sec:paper_eqs_explicit_eoms}) and then reproduced within the QuTiP framework to validate the Hamiltonian-based implementation against the paper reference. The QuTiP workflow is used as the main analysis tool, because it is defined directly in terms of Hamiltonians and bath models and can therefore be extended more systematically beyond the original paper formulations. In the monomer case, this additionally enables a Redfield treatment that is not included in the reference papers. The complete paper-equation comparison atlas is collected in~\autoref{app:paper_eqs_reference_atlas}.

\section{Monomer}
\label{sec:results_monomer}

A generic monomer in principle has an infinite ladder of excited states. Here, the strict limit considered reduces it to a two-level system and therefore to the simplest possible case in which the complete spectroscopy workflow can be validated. The monomer results are presented first in the 1D time domain ($t_{\mathrm{det}}$), then in its Fourier transform, then in the full $(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{det}})$ signal, and finally in the 2D spectra in $(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})$ space.

\subsection{Model and equations of motion}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_model_eom}

The monomer is the simplest case in which the full three-pulse workflow can be inspected. Its field-free Hamiltonian is, cf.~\cref{eq:spctr_tls_hamiltonian,eq:spctr_tls_dipole},
\begin{equation*}
\hat{H}_{0}^{(\mathrm{mon})}=\omega_{eg}\ket{e}\!\bra{e},
\end{equation*}
and the dipole operator is
\begin{equation*}
\hat{\mu}^{(\mathrm{mon})}=\mu_{ge}\ket{g}\!\bra{e}+\mu_{eg}\ket{e}\!\bra{g}.
\end{equation*}
The published phenomenology of Paper~I is characterised by a population decay rate $\gamma$ and a pure dephasing rate $\gamma_{\varphi}$, with $T_1=\gamma^{-1}$ and $T_2^{-1}=\gamma/2+\gamma_{\varphi}$. In the QuTiP implementation used here, the same phenomenology is written in Lindblad form with
\begin{equation*}
\hat{L}_{\varphi}=\sqrt{2\gamma_{\varphi}}\ket{e}\!\bra{e},
\qquad
\hat{L}_{\downarrow}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\downarrow}}\ket{g}\!\bra{e},
\qquad
\hat{L}_{\uparrow}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\uparrow}}\ket{e}\!\bra{g}.
\end{equation*}
The identification with the notation of Paper~I is therefore $\gamma_{\downarrow}\equiv\gamma$. The printed paper phenomenology does not introduce an upward thermal excitation channel, so in the strict calibration against the paper one sets $\gamma_{\uparrow}=0$. Physically, this corresponds to the zero-temperature limit of the phenomenological bath, where detailed balance suppresses upward transitions and only relaxation from $\ket{e}$ to $\ket{g}$ remains. Keeping $\gamma_{\uparrow}$ explicit is nevertheless useful because it shows how the phenomenological two-level model would be extended to a finite-temperature bath even before switching to a microscopic Redfield description. The explicit rotating-wave equations used by the phenomenological calibration backend are collected in~\autoref{app:sec:paper_eqs_explicit_eoms}. \todoimp{das stimmt nicht mehr ->} In the main text, they serve as the reference against which the QuTiP implementations are judged.

For real samples, the measured response reflects an ensemble of local environments rather than a single perfectly identical monomer, so the spectra are evaluated over many static-disorder realisations, see~\autoref{spctr:sec:macroscopic_polarisation}. The transition frequency is sampled from the Gaussian distribution introduced in~\autoref{eq:gaussian_broadening}. The spectra are then obtained from the disorder-averaged signal defined in~\autoref{eq:averaged_response}. The parameter $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}$ denotes the corresponding full width at half maximum of the distribution. In the homogeneous case, $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0$, all realisations share the same transition frequency and the linewidth is determined by dynamical dephasing and relaxation. In the inhomogeneous case, static disorder adds a spread of transition frequencies across the ensemble, exactly the distinction emphasised already in~\autoref{sec:spctr_spectroscopy_motivation}.

All monomer runs discussed in the main text use $\tilde{\nu}_{eg}=\SI{16000}{\per\centi\metre}$, $\mu_{ge}=1$, resonant excitation, and pulse amplitudes $(0.002,0.002,0.002)$. In units with $\hbar=1$, the quantity $\mu_{eg}E(t)$ has units of inverse time and corresponds to the instantaneous Rabi frequency, while the choice $\mu_{ge}=1$ fixes the field-amplitude scale used throughout this section. For the phenomenological benchmark, the fitted rates are $\gamma_{\varphi}=0.01~\si{\per\femto\second}$, $\gamma_{\downarrow}=1/300~\si{\per\femto\second}$, and $\gamma_{\uparrow}=0$. Static disorder, when included, uses $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=\SI{200}{\per\centi\metre}$.  \todoimp{das stimmt nicht mehr ->}The appendix additionally documents a supplementary Fig.~4 sensitivity set with stronger pulses $(0.005,0.005,0.005)$.

\subsection{1D time-domain response}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_1d_time}

At fixed coherence and waiting times, the quantity considered here is a one-dimensional slice through the phase-selected time-domain signals
$E_{\mathrm{R/NR}}(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}})$ introduced in~\autoref{eq:time_domain_signal}.
\autoref{fig:results_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom} reproduces the homogeneous (left panels) and inhomogeneous monomer cases (right panels) discussed in Paper~I~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}. It shows the corresponding time-dependent photon-echo benchmark for $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=\SI{200}{\per\centi\metre}$, and resolves the signal into its real and imaginary parts, while also showing both the rephasing and nonrephasing branches. The distinction between these two branches, introduced in~\autoref{subsec:spctr_rephasing_nonrephasing}, can be read off directly from the upper and lower rows.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.2\linewidth]{figures/results_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom}
    \caption{Monomer 1D time-domain traces at fixed $t_{\mathrm{coh}}=\SI{300}{\femto\second}$ and $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{1000}{\femto\second}$.
    The traces were obtained both from the explicit paper equations of motion and from the calibrated QuTiP workflow. Since the two methods yield visually equivalent plots, only one representative set is shown.
    The columns show the real, imaginary, and absolute signal components as functions of $t_{\mathrm{det}}$.
    The upper row shows the rephasing signal and the lower row the nonrephasing signal.
    Left: homogeneous ensemble, $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0$.
    Right: inhomogeneous ensemble, $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=\SI{200}{\per\centi\metre}$, averaged over $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=10000$ disorder realisations.}
    \label{fig:results_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom}
\end{figure}

In the homogeneous case, all monomers share the same transition frequency. The rephasing and nonrephasing traces are equivalent. In both rows, the real part decays smoothly with detection time, and the imaginary part remains zero for the chosen phase convention. The signal shows the expected free-induction decay.

The right-hand part shows the inhomogeneous ensemble with $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=\SI{200}{\per\centi\metre}$. Here, the difference between the two phase-matching branches becomes explicit. In the rephasing case, shown in the upper row, the signal is initially suppressed and then develops a pronounced delayed maximum near $t_{\mathrm{det}}\approx t_{\mathrm{coh}}$.\footnote{The short-time part of the inhomogeneous rephasing trace is not in perfect point-by-point agreement with the corresponding panel in Paper~I. This is not a concern: even $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=10000$ still represents a finite ensemble rather than a truly macroscopic limit, and the paper does not report the exact value of $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}$. The dependence on this sampling parameter is shown explicitly in~\autoref{fig:app_paper_eqs_monomer_fig2_time}. Already at $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=100$ the photon echo is clearly resolved, which is why $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=100$ is used as a practical default in the inhomogeneous reruns from now on.}
This is the characteristic photon echo: the phase reversal of the rephasing branch compensates the static frequency spread and refocuses the ensemble. In the nonrephasing case, shown in the lower row, no such refocusing occurs. The signal remains concentrated at short detection times and subsequently decays, in agreement with the theory.

The peak ratio $\mathrm{R}/\mathrm{NR}$ quantifies the difference between homogeneous and inhomogeneous behaviour: it remains $1.0$ in the homogeneous case and increases to $1.45$ in the inhomogeneous case. This shows how the photon-echo refocusing in the rephasing branch becomes dominant once static disorder is introduced. Both 1D benchmarks were also reproduced with the explicit equations of motion alongside the QuTiP implementation used in this work. Their agreement validates the QuTiP formulation for the monomer. All subsequent results are therefore generated with the QuTiP-based implementation because it can be extended straightforwardly to larger molecular systems.

The branch balance changes more strongly than in the time trace, with the peak ratio rising from $\mathrm{R}/\mathrm{NR}=1.00$ in the homogeneous slice to $2.52$ in the inhomogeneous one.

\subsection{2D time-domain response}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_2d_time}

Now, the coherence time between the first and second pulses is also varied. The signal is therefore resolved as
$E_{\mathrm{R/NR}}(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}})$
at fixed waiting time $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.2\linewidth]{figures/results_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom}
    \caption{Monomer 2D time-domain traces at $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}$, $\SI{20}{\femto\second}$, and $\SI{40}{\femto\second}$.
    Left column: homogeneous case, $\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0$.

\autoref{fig:results_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom} shows the full dependence on coherence time and detection time. In the homogeneous case, shown in the left column, both the rephasing and nonrephasing signals remain concentrated close to the origin and decay with increasing $t_{\mathrm{coh}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{det}}$.

The inhomogeneous case, shown in the right column, changes specifically in the rephasing branch. There, the real part develops an elongated feature along $t_{\mathrm{det}} \approx t_{\mathrm{coh}}$. This is the delayed photon echo in the time domain. The nonrephasing branch does not show such refocusing. It stays localised near the origin and decays with increasing detection time.

The monomer does not show waiting-time dynamics in this model, as the signals appear identical at different waiting times. A finite pulse-overlap effect is discussed in the next subsection.

\label{subsec:results_monomer_2d_freq}

The spectra defined in~\autoref{eq:SR_def,eq:SNR_def}, as well as the absorptive combination, are shown. The left column shows the homogeneous case, while the right column shows the inhomogeneous case. The rows show the rephasing, nonrephasing, and absorptive spectra, respectively.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.2\linewidth]{figures/results_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom}
    \caption{Monomer 2D frequency-domain waiting-time series obtained at $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}$, \SI{20}{\femto\second}, and \SI{40}{\femto\second}.     \label{fig:results_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom}

To compare the spectra directly with Figs.~3 and 4 of Paper~I, mainly the first row of each $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$ panel in the present figure is relevant. The comparison is not exact because the plotting style differs: in Paper~I the spectra are shown as contour plots, so signal levels below 5\% are not displayed, whereas in the colour plots used here weaker trends can still be recognised.

For $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}$, the rephasing spectra agree well in their overall structure. In both columns, the real part has a positive central region with its maximum at the resonance frequency. In the present spectra, both the real and imaginary parts appear symmetric along the diagonal, while in the paper the signal is stretched more strongly towards the $\omega_{\mathrm{det}}$ axis. When comparing the three waiting times, one further visual difference appears in the imaginary part of Paper~I: the axis separating positive and negative regions seems to become less tilted with increasing waiting time, whereas in the present figure the nodal line remains approximately diagonal.

In the present data, the corresponding short-$t_{\mathrm{wait}}$ phase twist is most clearly visible only in the absorptive spectrum at $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}$. The phase twist is a known pulse-overlap effect that arises when the first two pulses overlap in time. The system then has no time to evolve fully into a population state before the third pulse arrives. As the waiting time increases, the phase twist diminishes, leading to more symmetric spectra. In the homogeneous case, the frequency-domain branch ratios return to values close to unity once the pulse-overlap regime is passed.

The diagonal elongation in the inhomogeneous case is the central 2D signature of static disorder. As described in~\autoref{sec:spctr_spectroscopy_motivation}, the spectrum can be understood as a superposition of many homogeneous spectra shifted along the diagonal by realisation-dependent transition frequencies. The diagonal width therefore grows with disorder, whereas the antidiagonal width continues to reflect the homogeneous dephasing. As in the homogeneous case, the visible waiting-time evolution is largely confined to the pulse-overlap regime. Unlike in the 2D time-domain maps, however, the frequency-domain peak ratios remain clearly above unity even after pulse overlap, so the rephasing branch stays visibly dominant in the inhomogeneous spectra. This again indicates that the photon echo remains the dominant contribution to the total signal.

\subsection{Microscopic monomer extension}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_redfield}

After the phenomenological Lindblad baseline has been calibrated and interpreted across all four representations, the next step is to replace fitted rates by an explicit bath model. The monomer is again the cleanest place to do this, because the microscopic change in the open-system description can be separated from any complications due to multilevel spectral structure.

\subsection{Monomer summary}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_summary}

The monomer results are deliberately organised in the same order as the signal construction itself. The 1D time-domain traces show the basic photon-echo logic: no delayed echo in the homogeneous case, but a clear rephasing echo once static disorder is introduced. The 1D frequency-domain traces then show the corresponding line-shape contrast, while the 2D time-domain maps make the echo feature visible before Fourier transformation and the 2D frequency-domain spectra turn this into the compact homogeneous peak and the diagonally elongated inhomogeneous peak familiar from Paper~I.

The branch-ratio summary sharpens that picture. Static disorder makes the rephasing contribution systematically stronger than the nonrephasing one, but the clarity of that statement depends on the representation. Peak heights are already useful in 1D and become especially informative in 2D frequency space, where the inhomogeneous rephasing branch remains about $2.7$ times larger than the nonrephasing branch after pulse overlap. In 2D time space, by contrast, the decisive signature is the shape of the echo feature, not a much larger peak amplitude. Because the monomer has no cross peaks and no genuine waiting-time redistribution dynamics, it serves as the clean calibration case in which broadening physics can be separated from pulse-overlap effects before moving to more structured systems.

\section{Dimer}
\label{sec:results_dimer}

After the monomer benchmark, the next step is the dimer, i.e. two coupled monomers. Within the rotating-wave approximation, this is the simplest aggregate that includes one- and two-exciton states and can show off-diagonal cross peaks and waiting-time-dependent spectral changes. The discussion follows Paper~II~\cite{pisliakovetal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}, starting from two uncoupled monomers with different transition frequencies before turning to the coupled case. The section first establishes the uncoupled and coupled signals at $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=0$ and then interprets the signals over different waiting-times. The aim is to show how coupling-induced features appear in the spectra and how they evolve at later waiting times.
For the dimer, the explicit equations of motion in~\autoref{app:sec:paper_eqs_explicit_eoms} are already microscopically derived. In the main text, only the corresponding QuTiP implementation is shown, because the later extension to larger systems is built on that workflow. The explicit paper-equation runs were checked separately and reproduce the same features, so the plotted dimer panels can be read as the QuTiP realisation of the same microscopic model.


\subsection{Model}
\label{subsec:results_dimer_model_eom}

First, the model is introduced.
In the site basis $\{\ket{g},\ket{A},\ket{B},\ket{f}\equiv\ket{AB}\}$, the field-free Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation*}
\hat{H}_{0}^{(\mathrm{dim})}
=
\omega_{A}\ket{A}\!\bra{A}
+
\omega_{B}\ket{B}\!\bra{B}
+
J\bigl(\ket{A}\!\bra{B}+\ket{B}\!\bra{A}\bigr)
+
(\omega_{A}+\omega_{B})\ket{f}\!\bra{f},
\end{equation*}
where $\omega_{A}$ and $\omega_{B}$ are the site energies of the two monomers and $J$ is the coupling between them. To interpret the spectra, it is convenient to switch to the exciton basis. In this basis, $\ket{0}=\ket{g}$ and $\ket{3}=\ket{AB}$, while $\ket{1}$ and $\ket{2}$ are the two $J$-mixed one-exciton states. Only the one-exciton block is diagonalised.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/rslts_dimer_site_vs_exciton_basis}
    \caption{Basis map used in the dimer section. Left: site basis with local states $\ket{A}$ and $\ket{B}$, site dipoles $\mu_A$ and $\mu_B$, and coupling $J$. Middle: only the one-exciton block is diagonalised, so $\ket{0}=\ket{g}$ and $\ket{3}=\ket{AB}$ stay unchanged. Right: exciton basis with mixed states $\ket{1}$ and $\ket{2}$ and the adjacent-manifold transitions $\mu_{10}$, $\mu_{20}$, $\mu_{31}$, and $\mu_{32}$. The figure is adapted from Paper~II.}
    \label{fig:results_dimer_basis_map}
\end{figure}
\autoref{fig:results_dimer_basis_map} shows the two bases on an energy scale. In the site basis, the dipole operator allows transitions between the ground state and the two local excited states, with dipole moments $\mu_A$ and $\mu_B$. In the exciton basis, this becomes coupling between adjacent manifolds through the transitions $\mu_{10}$, $\mu_{20}$, $\mu_{31}$, and $\mu_{32}$. The direct transition between the double-exciton state and the ground state remains dipole-forbidden.


The parameters quoted in this subsection are used in the entire dimer analysis. The pulse duration is \SI{5}{\femto\second}. In this setting, the positive- and negative-frequency parts remain separated by $\omega=0$, so the rotating-wave approximation is expected to hold. The pulse-carrier frequency is $\omega_L=\SI{16000}{\per\centi\metre}$, at the centre of the one-exciton manifold. For the dimer, the coupling is set so that the diagonal-peak separation matches the uncoupled transition difference. The later waiting-time panels follow the regenerated Paper-II atlas settings and are discussed separately in the corresponding subsection.

\section{Towards the general \texorpdfstring{$N$}{N}-site extension}
\label{sec:results_nsite_extension}

The numerical workflow developed in this chapter is not restricted to the monomer and dimer. At the level of the implementation described in~\autoref{app:numerical_implementation}, the same sequence remains available for a general $N$-site excitonic model: construct the Hamiltonian and dipole operators in the truncated excitation manifolds, propagate under the three-pulse field, isolate the desired phase-matched contribution by phase cycling, average over static disorder, and Fourier transform the resulting signal into $(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})$ space.

What remains unfinished in the present thesis is not the basic simulation machinery, but the final production of a larger, fully interpreted figure set. The next benchmarks should therefore be staged deliberately: first a trimer sanity check, then a short linear chain, then a ring geometry, and only then a tiny cylinder. This ordering keeps the spectra interpretable while preserving the longer-term goal of extending the workflow toward microtubule-inspired aggregates.
