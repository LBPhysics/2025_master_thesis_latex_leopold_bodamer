\chapter{Introduction}
\label{Chapter_Introduction}

\noindent
Ultrafast optical spectroscopy provides a direct experimental window into quantum dynamics on femtosecond timescales. In many molecular and condensed-matter systems, the relevant degrees of freedom are not isolated: they are embedded in complex environments that induce relaxation and dephasing, and can strongly reshape transport and coherence signatures. This interplay is particularly important in ``hot'' settings (room temperature, soft-matter, biological complexes), where one expects fast decoherence but still observes structured spectral features that encode microscopic couplings and dynamical pathways.

\medskip
\noindent
Two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy (2DES) was developed precisely to disentangle such effects. By correlating excitation and detection frequencies while controlling the delays between ultrashort pulses, 2DES separates homogeneous from inhomogeneous broadening and reveals couplings and energy-transfer pathways via diagonal and cross peaks \cite{jonas2003twodimensionalfemtosecondspectroscopy, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, gelzinisetal2019twodimensionalspectroscopynonspecialists}. On the theory side, the same observables can be expressed in terms of multi-time response functions of the dipole operator, which naturally connect spectroscopy to open-quantum-system propagators \cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}.

\section{Scope and approach}
\label{sec:intro_scope}

\noindent
The goal of this thesis is to build a consistent modelling workflow for 2DES signals of open quantum systems in regimes relevant to warm, structured environments. The emphasis is on a pragmatic weak-coupling description that connects microscopic bath models (via correlation functions / spectral densities) to relaxation and dephasing rates, while remaining computationally feasible for parameter scans and multi-site systems.

\medskip
\noindent
We therefore use the Redfield master equation as the default open-system generator \cite{breuerpetruccione2009theoryopenquantum, weiss2012quantumdissipativesystems, rivasetal2010markovianmasterequations}. Redfield theory provides a transparent bridge from a system--bath Hamiltonian to dissipative dynamics, but it is not guaranteed to be completely positive; throughout, its use is restricted to regimes where the weak-coupling and Markov assumptions are well-motivated and results are interpreted with appropriate care. For comparison and for controlled limiting cases, Lindblad-form models are used where appropriate.

\medskip
\noindent
On the spectroscopy side, we follow the standard third-order response-function framework of nonlinear optics \cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy}. In addition, the numerical implementation used in this thesis adopts a non-perturbative (time-domain) strategy in which the driven open-system equation of motion is solved with the pulses explicitly included, and lower-order background contributions are removed by auxiliary runs. This provides direct access to complex-valued signals via phase cycling and enables a faithful treatment of finite pulse durations and pulse overlap effects \cite{segarra-martietal2018accuratesimulationtwodimensional, sunetal2024twodimensionalspectroscopyopen}.

\section{Model systems and physical questions}
\label{sec:intro_models_questions}

\noindent
To connect the formalism to interpretable signatures in 2D spectra, the analysis proceeds from minimal reference models to structured ensembles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Two-level system (TLS):} a baseline to validate the numerical workflow and to separate homogeneous dephasing, population relaxation and static disorder through their distinct 2D line-shape fingerprints.
    \item \textbf{Dimer:} the minimal model that produces cross peaks and allows one to contrast coherent coupling (excitonic mixing) with incoherent population transfer during the waiting time.
    \item \textbf{$N$-site ensembles with geometry:} larger systems in which 2D spectra can visualize geometry-dependent coupling networks and transport. Motivated by protofilament arrangements in microtubules, a discretized cylindrical architecture is used as a concrete, structured test case \cite{kalraetal2023electronicenergymigration}.
\end{itemize}
The central question throughout is how environment-induced relaxation and dephasing compete with coherent couplings to shape experimentally accessible observables: peak positions, line shapes, and waiting-time dynamics.
