\chapter{Results}
\label{chapter:results}

This chapter presents the results for the models defined in~\autoref{chapter:model_systems}. It starts with the monomer, then turns to the dimer, and finally shows selected examples for larger systems with \(N\) sites.

Common implementation details are collected in~\autoref{app:numerical_implementation}. For the benchmark cases, the spectra were first reconstructed from the equations of motion collected in~\autoref{app:sec:paper_eqs_explicit_eoms} and then reproduced with the general \texttt{QuTiP} framework. Only the \texttt{QuTiP} results are shown in the main text. Additional comparison figures are collected in~\autoref{app:supplementary_comparison_figures}.

Unless stated otherwise, figures follow a common layout.
Within each panel, the columns show the real part, imaginary part, and absolute
value of the displayed signal. Time-domain panels show the rephasing signal in
the first row and the nonrephasing signal in the second row. Frequency-domain
panels that include all branches add the absorptive spectrum as a third row,
defined in~\autoref{eq:Sab_def}. In contour plots, levels start at \(5\%\) of
the absolute signal and continue in steps of \(10\%\); solid contours indicate
positive values and dashed contours negative values. Axis labels and, where
present, colour-bar labels apply to all axes and colour bars in the
corresponding figure. Deviations from these
conventions are stated in the corresponding captions.

\section{Monomer}
\label{sec:results_monomer}
The monomer results use the phenomenological Lindblad model introduced in~\autoref{subsec:model_tls_lindblad}. They are presented in four steps: first the 1D time-domain response as a function of \(t_{\mathrm{det}}\), then its Fourier transform, then the full \((t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{det}})\) signal, and finally the 2D spectra in \((\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})\) space.

\subsection{Setup for the Present Figures}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_model}

For all monomer figures in the main text (\crefrange{fig:rslts_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom}{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom_rephasing}), the system is a single optical transition with \(\omega_{\mathrm{eg}}=\SI{16000}{\per\centi\metre}\), dipole strength \(\mu_{\mathrm{ge}}=1\), and resonant excitation \(\omega_{\mathrm{L}}=\omega_{\mathrm{eg}}\). The laser is modelled by three Gaussian pulses with \(\tau_{\mathrm{p}}=\SI{15}{\femto\second}\) FWHM and amplitudes \((0.002,0.002,0.002)\), as described in~\autoref{app:sec:laser_field}. The effective Lindblad rates are \(\gamma_{\varphi}=1/100~\si{\per\femto\second}\), \(\gamma_{\downarrow}=1/300~\si{\per\femto\second}\), and \(\gamma_{\uparrow}=0\). These correspond to the pure-dephasing and relaxation timescales \(T_{2}^{*}=\SI{100}{\femto\second}\) and \(T_{1}=\SI{300}{\femto\second}\), respectively. The optical-coherence decay rate is \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}=\gamma_{\varphi}+(\gamma_{\downarrow}+\gamma_{\uparrow})/2=7/600~\si{\per\femto\second}\), so that \(T_{2}=1/\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}\approx\SI{85.7}{\femto\second}\). This corresponds to the homogeneous wavenumber half-width \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}\approx\SI{61.9}{\per\centi\metre}\). The inhomogeneous broadening is set to \(\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=\SI{200}{\per\centi\metre}\) when present. For the inhomogeneous 1D monomer figures, \(N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=10000\), \(t_{\mathrm{coh}}=\SI{300}{\femto\second}\), \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{1000}{\femto\second}\), \(t_{\mathrm{det}}\in[0,\SI{600}{\femto\second}]\), and \(\Delta t=\SI{2}{\femto\second}\). For the inhomogeneous 2D monomer figures, \(N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=100\), \(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{det}}\in[0,\SI{600}{\femto\second}]\), \(\Delta t=\SI{2}{\femto\second}\), and \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second},\SI{20}{\femto\second},\SI{40}{\femto\second}\). The smaller 2D ensemble is a practical compromise to keep the computational cost manageable while still clearly resolving the photon echo feature in the 2D time-domain maps. Only figure-specific deviations from these defaults are stated in the corresponding captions.

\subsection{1D Time-Domain Signal}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_1d_time}

At fixed coherence and waiting times, the signal is a one-dimensional slice of \(E_{\mathrm{R/NR}}(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}})\), introduced in~\autoref{eq:time_domain_signal}. \autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom} shows the homogeneous and inhomogeneous monomer signals. The upper and lower rows show different detection-time behaviour.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
  \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.22\linewidth]{figures/rslts_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom}
\caption{Monomer 1D time-domain traces. Shared settings are listed
in~\autoref{subsec:results_monomer_model}. (a): homogeneous ensemble,
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0\). (b): inhomogeneous
ensemble.}
    \label{fig:rslts_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom}
\end{figure}

In the homogeneous case, all monomers share the same transition frequency. The rephasing and nonrephasing traces are therefore equivalent. The real part decays smoothly with detection time, while the imaginary part remains zero for the chosen phase convention. No delayed echo appears.

In the inhomogeneous ensemble, the difference between the two phase-matching signals becomes explicit. In the rephasing case, shown in the upper row, the signal is initially suppressed and then develops a pronounced delayed maximum near $t_{\mathrm{det}}\approx t_{\mathrm{coh}}$.\footnote{The short-time part of the inhomogeneous rephasing trace is not in perfect agreement with the corresponding panel in~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}. This is not a concern because $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=10000$ is still a finite ensemble. That paper does not report the exact value of $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}$. The dependence on this sampling parameter is shown explicitly in~\autoref{fig:app_paper_eqs_monomer_fig2_time_n_dep}. However, the photon echo is already clearly resolved at $N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=100$.} This is the characteristic photon echo. Static inhomogeneity is essential for this delayed rephasing signal to appear. In the nonrephasing case, shown in the lower row, no such refocusing occurs. The signal remains concentrated at short detection times and then decays.

The peak ratio \(\mathrm{R}/\mathrm{NR}\) remains \(1.0\) in the homogeneous
case and increases to \(1.45\) in the inhomogeneous case. Thus, static disorder
enhances the rephasing signal through photon-echo refocusing.


\subsection{1D Frequency-Domain Spectrum}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_1d_freq}
This section Fourier transforms the time-domain signals in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom} along the detection-time axis. This frequency-domain slice is not shown in~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}. The resulting spectra are shown in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_1d_freq_hom_inhom}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
  \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.22\linewidth]{figures/rslts_monomer_1d_freq_hom_inhom}
\caption{Monomer 1D frequency-domain spectra for the time-domain signals
in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_1d_time_hom_inhom}. Shared settings are listed
in~\autoref{subsec:results_monomer_model}. (a): homogeneous ensemble.
(b): inhomogeneous ensemble.}
    \label{fig:rslts_monomer_1d_freq_hom_inhom}
\end{figure}

The influence of \(N_{\mathrm{inhom}}\) is collected in~\autoref{fig:app_paper_eqs_monomer_fig2_freq_n_dep}. In the homogeneous case, all monomers oscillate at the same transition frequency, so the spectral weight remains concentrated around \(\omega_{\mathrm{eg}}=\SI{16000}{\per\centi\metre}\). Rephasing and nonrephasing are then identical. Compared with the linear-susceptibility convention in~\autoref{spctr:sec:freq_domain_1d_response}, the absorptive and dispersive assignments are exchanged: here the real part is absorptive and the imaginary part is dispersive. This is only a phasing convention.

In the inhomogeneous case, the ensemble average collects many slightly shifted single-molecule responses, as discussed in~\autoref{sec:spctr_spectroscopy_motivation}. The transition is therefore broadened. The rephasing spectrum remains narrower than the nonrephasing one because refocusing compresses the rephasing weight into a sharper feature. The peak ratio rises from \(\mathrm{R}/\mathrm{NR}=1.00\) in the homogeneous slice to \(2.52\) in the inhomogeneous one.

A compact linewidth check is useful before moving to the two-dimensional spectra. The FWHM values in~\autoref{tab:results_monomer_1d_linewidths} were extracted from the absolute signal spectra, \(|\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{R/NR}}(\omega)|\).

The homogeneous value of \(\SI{213.53}{\per\centi\metre}\) is consistent with the absolute value of the complex homogeneous line shape. For \(g(\omega)\propto(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}-i\Delta)^{-1}\), the measured magnitude is \mbox{\(|g|\propto(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}^{2}+\Delta^{2})^{-1/2}\)}. Its half-maximum condition, \(|g(\Delta)|=|g(0)|/2\), gives \(\Delta^{2}=3\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}^{2}\) and therefore \(\mathrm{FWHM}_{|g|}=2\sqrt{3}\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}\approx\SI{214}{\per\centi\metre}\). The absorptive Lorentzian component would instead give \(2\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}\approx\SI{124}{\per\centi\metre}\) (see~\autoref{spctr:sec:freq_domain_1d_response}). The inhomogeneous 1D values show: the rephasing signal is narrow, whereas the nonrephasing signal broadens.

\subsection{2D Time-Domain Signal}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_2d_time}

The coherence time between the first and second pulses is now varied as well.
At fixed waiting time \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}\), the signal is therefore resolved as
\(E_{\mathrm{R/NR}}(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}})\).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.22\linewidth]{figures/rslts_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom_t000}
    \caption{Monomer 2D time-domain traces at
    \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}\). Shared settings are listed
    in~\autoref{subsec:results_monomer_model}. (a): homogeneous case,
    \(\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0\). (b):
    inhomogeneous case.}
    \label{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom}
\end{figure}

\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom} shows the full dependence on
coherence time and detection time at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=0\). In panel~(a),
corresponding to the homogeneous case, both the rephasing and nonrephasing
signals remain concentrated near the origin and decay with increasing
\(t_{\mathrm{coh}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{det}}\). No delayed echo feature is
visible.

In panel~(b), corresponding to the inhomogeneous case, the change appears in
the rephasing signal. There, the real part and the absolute value develop an
elongated feature along \(t_{\mathrm{det}}\approx t_{\mathrm{coh}}\), which is
the delayed photon echo in the time domain. By contrast, the nonrephasing
signal in panel~(b) remains localised near the origin and does not show such
refocusing.

Additional traces for larger waiting times can be found in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom_t020_040}. There,
panels~(a) and~(b) show the homogeneous and inhomogeneous cases at
\(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{20}{\femto\second}\), while panels~(c) and~(d)\ show
the corresponding cases at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{40}{\femto\second}\).
Within the visual resolution of these plots, panels~(a)--(d) show the same
time-domain structure as in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom}. No
clear waiting-time dependence is visible for the monomer in these time-domain
data. A short-time pulse-overlap contribution becomes apparent only in the
frequency-domain representation, as discussed in the next subsection.

\subsection{2D Frequency-Domain Spectrum}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_2d_freq}

The 2D frequency-domain response is shown
in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom_rephasing}. The spectra are
obtained by Fourier transforming the time-domain signals discussed
in~\autoref{subsec:results_monomer_2d_time}. The nonrephasing and absorptive branches are moved
to~\cref{fig:app_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom_nonrephasing,fig:app_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom_absorptive}
to keep the main-text comparison focused on the clearest visual signature. They are discussed quantitatively in the appendix, where the signal-resolved diagonal and anti-diagonal linewidths are collected.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
  \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.22\linewidth]{figures/rslts_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom_rephasing}
\caption{Monomer 2D frequency-domain waiting-time series, rephasing
component, for the time-domain signals in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom}
and~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_time_hom_inhom_t020_040}. Shared settings are listed
in~\autoref{subsec:results_monomer_model}. The waiting times are
\(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second},\SI{20}{\femto\second},\SI{40}{\femto\second}\). (a):

    \label{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom_rephasing}
\end{figure}

For comparison with Figures~3 and~4 of~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}, the real and imaginary parts
are the relevant panels, as the paper does not show the absolute-value plot. In the present figure, additional colouring is included
to make the continuous signal easier to identify.

In both the homogeneous and inhomogeneous cases, the real part has a positive
central region with its maximum at the resonance frequency. The main visible
change between different waiting times occurs
between $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}$ and
$\SI{20}{\femto\second}$, where the real parts of the spectra become less pronounced in the
$\omega_{\mathrm{det}}$ direction and more extended along the $\omega_{\mathrm{det}}$ axis.
This short-time distortion is a pulse-overlap
effect: when the first two pulses still overlap, the system does not evolve
fully into a population state before the third pulse arrives. Additional pulse orderings, such as 2-3-1, may also contribute to the signal. These overlap contributions quickly disappear as $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$ increases. This
short-$t_{\mathrm{wait}}$ dependence is more clearly visible in the absorptive
spectrum at $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}$, which is defined as the
sum of the real parts of the rephasing and nonrephasing signals,
see~\autoref{eq:Sab_def}.

In the present spectra, the real part shows a
negative region at all waiting times, whereas in~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse} this is shown only for
$t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}$ and vanishes for
$t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{20}{\femto\second}$ and
$\SI{40}{\femto\second}$. The overall structure still agrees
well with~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}, but some differences remain. In the present spectra, the
negative contour region in the real part is larger, and the imaginary part
remains more symmetric about the diagonal. The nodal line in the imaginary part
also stays diagonal over the full waiting-time series, whereas in~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse} the
nodal line in the homogeneous signals becomes less tilted with
increasing waiting time. The imaginary part in
~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}, in both the homogeneous and inhomogeneous signals, appears more strongly stretched towards the
$\omega_{\mathrm{det}}$ axis, which is not the case in the present figure.
The reference calculation~\cite{mancaletal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse} also shows a visible evolution between
$t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{20}{\femto\second}$ and
$\SI{40}{\femto\second}$, which is not resolved in the present results. For
longer times, $t_{\mathrm{wait}} > 2\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$, it was checked that the
homogeneous line shape does not change further.

In the inhomogeneous case, the central 2D signature is the diagonal elongation
of the peak. As described in~\autoref{sec:spctr_spectroscopy_motivation}, the
spectrum can be understood as a superposition of many homogeneous spectra
shifted along the diagonal by the realisation-dependent transition frequency.
The diagonal width therefore increases with disorder, whereas the anti-diagonal
width remains closer to the homogeneous coherence width. This can be made
quantitative by taking diagonal and anti-diagonal cuts through the maximum of
the rephasing signal shown in~\autoref{fig:rslts_monomer_2d_freq_hom_inhom_rephasing}.
The resulting FWHM values are listed
in~\autoref{tab:results_monomer_2d_rephasing_linewidths}.

The homogeneous rephasing widths provide a direct check of the imposed
homogeneous dephasing rate. They are nearly isotropic and remain close to
\(\SI{175}{\per\centi\metre}\) for all three waiting times, in excellent
agreement with the expected
\(2\sqrt{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}\approx\SI{175}{\per\centi\metre}\) for a
product-like homogeneous signal magnitude. This factor follows from the geometry of the cut: locally, a homogeneous signal magnitude has the form \(|\tilde{E}|\propto |g(\Delta_{\mathrm{coh}})|\,|g(\Delta_{\mathrm{det}})|\). Along a normalized diagonal or anti-diagonal coordinate \(u\), both detunings change as \(u/\sqrt{2}\), so \(|\tilde{E}(u)|/|\tilde{E}(0)|=\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}^{2}/(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}^{2}+u^{2}/2)\). Setting this ratio to \(1/2\) gives \(|u|=\sqrt{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}\), hence \(\mathrm{FWHM}=2\sqrt{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{eg}}\). Once static disorder is included,
this simple homogeneous reference no longer applies directly: both the diagonal
and anti-diagonal rephasing widths become much larger. The diagonal width grows
to about \(\SI{414}{\per\centi\metre}\), while the anti-diagonal width is about
\(\SI{269}{\per\centi\metre}\). The inhomogeneous spectrum is therefore
broadened in both directions, but remains more strongly elongated along the
diagonal, where the distribution of transition frequencies is collected. The
nonrephasing and absorptive linewidths are listed with their corresponding
supplementary figures in~\autoref{tab:app_monomer_2d_branch_linewidths}.

Thus, the frequency-domain monomer results show two main points. First, static
disorder changes the spectrum primarily through diagonal elongation. Second,
the visible waiting-time dependence is confined to the short-time
pulse-overlap regime. Beyond that regime, no further qualitative change is
visible in the frequency-domain line shape of the static monomer model.

To complement the figure-based discussion, the relative peak amplitudes of the rephasing and nonrephasing signals are collected in~\autoref{tab:results_monomer_branch_ratios}. The table provides a compact check of signal dominance in each representation.

\subsection{Monomer Summary}
\label{subsec:results_monomer_summary}

The monomer results show two basic effects. First, the 1D time-domain traces verify the photon echo: the homogeneous case shows no delayed echo, while the inhomogeneous case does. Second, the frequency-domain traces show the corresponding line-shape contrast. The 2D spectra show the same effect as a compact homogeneous peak and a diagonally elongated inhomogeneous peak.

The signal-ratio summary supports this picture. Static disorder makes the rephasing contribution stronger than the nonrephasing contribution, especially in the 2D frequency domain. After the pulse-overlap regime, the inhomogeneous rephasing signal remains about \(2.7\) times larger than the nonrephasing signal. In the 2D time domain, the main signature is the echo shape rather than a much larger peak amplitude. The monomer is therefore the simplest case for separating broadening effects from pulse-overlap effects.

\section{Dimer}
\label{sec:results_dimer}

This section turns to the smallest coupled aggregate considered in this
thesis, the dimer.

\subsection{Setup for the Present Figures}
\label{subsec:results_dimer_model}

For all dimer figures in the main text
(\crefrange{fig:rslts_dimer_2d_time_un_coupled}{fig:rslts_dimer_fig6_homogeneous_companion}),
the characteristic optical scale defined
in~\autoref{eq:app_characteristic_optical_scale} is fixed to
\(\bar{\omega}_0=(\omega_{\mathrm{A}}+\omega_{\mathrm{B}})/2=\SI{16000}{\per\centi\metre}\).
The field is modelled by three Gaussian pulses with
\(\tau_{\mathrm{p}}=\SI{5}{\femto\second}\) FWHM, amplitudes
\((0.002,0.002,0.002)\), and carrier frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{L}}=\bar{\omega}_0\).
All 2D dimer figures use
\(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{det}}\in[0,\SI{600}{\femto\second}]\) and
\(\Delta t=\SI{2}{\femto\second}\). Common implementation details are
documented in~\autoref{app:numerical_implementation}.

Two dimer parameter sets are used in the main text. The inhomogeneous
comparison at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}\) uses
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=\SI{200}{\per\centi\metre}\) and
\(N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=10000\). The uncoupled dimer has
\((\omega_{\mathrm{A}},\omega_{\mathrm{B}})=(\SI{16360}{\per\centi\metre},\SI{15640}{\per\centi\metre})\),
\((\mu_{\mathrm{A}},\mu_{\mathrm{B}})=(1.0,1.0)\), and \(J=0\). The coupled
dimer has
\((\omega_{\mathrm{A}},\omega_{\mathrm{B}})=(\SI{15800}{\per\centi\metre},\SI{16200}{\per\centi\metre})\),
\((\mu_{\mathrm{A}},\mu_{\mathrm{B}})=(-0.23,1.0)\), and
\(J=\SI{300}{\per\centi\metre}\).

The coupled waiting-time sequence uses
\((\omega_{\mathrm{A}},\omega_{\mathrm{B}})=(\SI{16200}{\per\centi\metre},\SI{15800}{\per\centi\metre})\),
\((\mu_{\mathrm{A}},\mu_{\mathrm{B}})=(1.0,-0.15)\),
\(J=\SI{500}{\per\centi\metre}\),
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=\SI{200}{\per\centi\metre}\), and
\(N_{\mathrm{inhom}}=1000\). Open-system effects are treated with local,
mutually uncorrelated baths within the Redfield framework. The Ohmic bath
parameters are \(T=\SI{77}{\kelvin}\), \(s=1\),
\(\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=(3/67)\bar{\omega}_0\), and
\(\alpha=(7/109)\bar{\omega}_0\). 

Through the Redfield rates
in~\autoref{eq:app_dimer_redfield_rates}, these bath values correspond to
\(T_{2}=1/\Gamma_{12}\approx\SI{350}{\femto\second}\) for the one-exciton
coherence and \(T_{1}=1/\Gamma_{22}\approx\SI{200}{\femto\second}\) for
downhill population relaxation. These two timescales describe waiting-time
population and coherence dynamics. The spectral linewidths below are measured
from the simulated 2D response itself. The
homogeneous companion uses the same
coupled waiting-time parameters, except that static disorder is switched off,
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0\).


\subsection{Coupled Case at \texorpdfstring{$t_{\mathrm{wait}}=0$}{t wait = 0}}
\label{subsec:results_dimer_coupled_case}

The first comparison considers the inhomogeneous dimer at $t_{\mathrm{wait}}=0$. The aim is to compare the uncoupled and coupled two-transition patterns in the time and frequency domains.

\subsubsection{2D Time-Domain Signal}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
  \safeplaceholdergraphic[1.22\linewidth]{figures/rslts_dimer_2d_time_un_coupled}
  \caption{Dimer 2D time-domain signals of the inhomogeneous uncoupled and
  coupled dimers at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}\). Shared dimer
  settings are listed in~\autoref{subsec:results_dimer_model}. (a) uncoupled
  dimer. (b) coupled dimer.}
  \label{fig:rslts_dimer_2d_time_un_coupled}
\end{figure}

\autoref{fig:rslts_dimer_2d_time_un_coupled} shows the complex 2D photon-echo signal in the time domain at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=0\). Panel~(a) shows the uncoupled dimer, and panel~(b) shows the coupled dimer. Only the inhomogeneous signal is shown, because this is the relevant case for the macroscopic echo. The echo appears in both panels because static disorder is included.

The uncoupled dimer rephasing signal shows oscillations along the anti-diagonal. This is because the dimer contains two optical transitions with different frequencies, whereas the monomer contains only one. The real part remains dominant, but the imaginary part becomes visible.

In the lower row, the nonrephasing signal shows the corresponding oscillatory pattern with the opposite diagonal tilt.

In the coupled dimer case, the signal decays faster and develops more short-time structure. The time-domain maps still show visible amplitude near the largest simulated values of \(t_{\mathrm{coh}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{det}}\), especially near the upper-right edge of the plotted window. The finite time window therefore cuts off a signal that has not fully decayed. In the post-processing, this finite signal is padded with zeros before the Fourier transform, see~\autoref{app:sec:post_processing}. The cutoff can therefore introduce sharp edges in the numerical time-domain array. These finite-window effects may contribute to the small side structures around the sharp diagonal rephasing peaks in the frequency-domain spectra.
To determine which of these changes remain after the Fourier transform, the same signal is now analysed in the frequency domain.

\autoref{fig:rslts_dimer_2d_freq_un_coupled} shows the corresponding frequency-domain spectra. Compared with~\cite{pisliakovetal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse}, the features are narrower and more clearly separated. The finite-window effect discussed above is one possible factor, because it can change the line shape and add small side structures. A direct visual comparison with~\cite{pisliakovetal2006twodimensionalopticalthreepulse} is therefore difficult.

The uncoupled and coupled parameter sets have the same diagonal-peak separation. The comparison therefore isolates the spectral changes caused by coupling.

In the uncoupled case ($J=0$), the rephasing spectra show two diagonal peaks at $(\omega_{10},\omega_{10})$ and $(\omega_{20},\omega_{20})$. They correspond to two independent optical resonances: excitation at $\omega_{10}$ leads to emission at $\omega_{10}$, and excitation at $\omega_{20}$ leads to emission at $\omega_{20}$. The rephasing spectrum therefore remains concentrated around these two diagonal positions. The imaginary part shows the corresponding dispersive structure around the same resonances. The nonrephasing spectra are centred at the same diagonal frequencies, but the line shape is broader and more connected between the two peaks. Its imaginary part again shows the corresponding dispersive response at the two diagonal positions. The two diagonal resonances are almost balanced in the real rephasing spectrum, with \(|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{11}|/|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{22}|\approx 0.98\).

In the coupled case, two cross peaks appear in addition to the diagonal peaks. They are located at $(\omega_{10},\omega_{20})$ and $(\omega_{20},\omega_{10})$, and therefore show coupling between the one-exciton states. Peak $21$ means excitation near $\omega_{20}$ on the coherence axis and emission near $\omega_{10}$ on the detection axis. Equivalently, excitation at $\omega_{20}$ can, due to coupling, lead to emission at $\omega_{10}$. Peak $12$ is the opposite cross peak, with excitation near $\omega_{10}$ and emission near $\omega_{20}$. In the rephasing spectra, the diagonal peaks $11$ and $22$ remain dominant, while the cross peaks are resolved but weaker. In the nonrephasing spectra, the same four peak positions are visible, but the contour pattern is broader and more connected. The cross peaks are therefore less isolated in the nonrephasing branch. Coupling also changes the relative diagonal-peak amplitudes: in the real rephasing spectrum, \(|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{11}|/|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{22}|\approx 1.64\), whereas the uncoupled dimer is nearly balanced. Peak $22$ is weaker, and the cross peaks are comparatively faint. The cross-peak intensities should be read with care, because they depend on \(J\) and on the dipole strengths and orientations. The full waiting-time dependence is analysed below.

\subsection{Waiting-Time Dynamics}
\label{subsec:results_dimer_waiting_time}

\autoref{fig:rslts_dimer_fig4_waiting_time_rephasing} shows the rephasing waiting-time series of the coupled dimer. The panel at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}\) still lies in the finite-pulse overlap regime, so the discussion focuses on \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}>\SI{0}{\femto\second}\). In this range, the four resolved peaks \(11\), \(12\), \(21\), and \(22\) are analysed separately.

  \centering

 \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second},\SI{16}{\femto\second},\SI{30}{\femto\second},\SI{46}{\femto\second},\SI{62}{\femto\second},\SI{108}{\femto\second},\SI{140}{\femto\second},\SI{310}{\femto\second}\).

The local real-part peak values support this peak-by-peak reading. Excluding \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}\), the diagonal peak \(11\) stays almost constant: \(\Re\,\tilde{E}_{11}\) changes only from \(9.41\times10^{-3}\) to \(9.46\times10^{-3}\). The short-time modulation is mainly carried by the cross peaks. At \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{16}{\femto\second}\) and \(\SI{46}{\femto\second}\), both cross peaks are negative in the real part, but peak \(12\) is larger than \(21\). The ratios are \(|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{12}|/|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{21}| \approx 4.9\) and \(7.5\). At \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{30}{\femto\second}\) and \(\SI{62}{\femto\second}\), the sign pattern reverses. Peak \(21\) is then larger than \(12\), with \(|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{21}|/|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{12}| \approx 2.5\) and \(2.9\). The waiting-time modulation is therefore not a global change of the whole panel. It is an oscillatory redistribution between the two cross peaks.

The panels at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{46}{\femto\second}\) and \(\SI{62}{\femto\second}\) are described in detail. At \(\SI{46}{\femto\second}\), the spectral weight is concentrated mainly in the left column. The strongest features are the $11$ diagonal peak and the $12$ cross peak above it. Peaks $21$ and $22$ are present, but much weaker. In the real part, $11$ appears mainly positive, while $12$ appears mainly negative. The imaginary part shows the same left-right imbalance, with the left-column peaks clearly dominating.

At \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{62}{\femto\second}\), the dominant weight has shifted mainly into the bottom row. The strongest features are now $11$ and the $21$ cross peak, while the upper-row peaks $12$ and $22$ are much weaker. The pattern therefore changes from being dominated by the left column to being dominated by the bottom row. In the real part, the dominant peaks $11$ and $21$ now both appear mainly positive. In the imaginary part, the same intensity swap is visible, but with a different sign pattern: the dominant lower-row peaks appear mainly negative.

This behaviour shows that the waiting-time evolution contains more than monotonic decay. The spectra contain a damped modulation on a timescale set by the exciton splitting. For the coupled dimer, the one-exciton energies give
\begin{equation*}
\omega_{21} \equiv \omega_2-\omega_1 = \sqrt{(\omega_{\mathrm{A}}-\omega_{\mathrm{B}})^2+4J^2},
\end{equation*}
so an excited-state coherence between \(\ket{1}\) and \(\ket{2}\) evolves as~\cite{mancal2022nonlinearopticalspectroscopy}
\begin{equation*}
\rho_{12}(t_{\mathrm{wait}})=\rho_{12}(0)e^{-i\omega_{21}t_{\mathrm{wait}}}e^{-\Gamma_{12}t_{\mathrm{wait}}}.
\end{equation*}
The corresponding peak dynamics can therefore be represented by a
non-oscillatory contribution plus a damped oscillatory contribution,
\begin{equation*}
E_{\mathrm{peak}}(t_{\mathrm{wait}})=E_{0}+E_{\mathrm{osc}}\cos(\omega_{21}t_{\mathrm{wait}}+\phi)e^{-\Gamma_{12}t_{\mathrm{wait}}}.
\end{equation*}
In this expression, \(E_{0}\) is the non-oscillatory background of the local peak contribution, \(E_{\mathrm{osc}}\) is the oscillation amplitude, and \(\phi\) is a phase offset. The corresponding beat period is
\begin{equation*}
T_{\mathrm{beat}}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{21}}.
\end{equation*}
For the present parameters,
\((\omega_{\mathrm{A}},\omega_{\mathrm{B}})=(\SI{16200}{\per\centi\metre},\SI{15800}{\per\centi\metre})\)
and \(J=\SI{500}{\per\centi\metre}\), this gives
\(\omega_{21}\approx\SI{1077}{\per\centi\metre}\) and
\(T_{\mathrm{beat}}\approx\SI{31}{\femto\second}\). This agrees with the
short-time alternation of the cross peaks. The displayed waiting times were
chosen to sample this hierarchy. The delay series is therefore not evenly spaced:
\(\SI{16}{\femto\second}\approx T_{\mathrm{beat}}/2\),
\(\SI{30}{\femto\second}\approx T_{\mathrm{beat}}\),
\(\SI{46}{\femto\second}\approx 3T_{\mathrm{beat}}/2\),
\(\SI{62}{\femto\second}\approx 2T_{\mathrm{beat}}\),
\(\SI{108}{\femto\second}\approx 7T_{\mathrm{beat}}/2\), and
\(\SI{140}{\femto\second}\approx 9T_{\mathrm{beat}}/2\). Since \(T_{2}\) is
much longer than \(T_{\mathrm{beat}}\), these early panels resolve several
coherent phase changes before the electronic coherence is damped.

At longer waiting times, population transfer on the \(T_{1}\) scale becomes
visible as a gradual redistribution away from the upper diagonal peak \(22\)
and towards the cross peak \(21\). The final panel at
\(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{310}{\femto\second}\) is therefore a late-time point:
it is longer than \(T_{1}\approx\SI{200}{\femto\second}\) and comparable to
\(T_{2}\approx\SI{350}{\femto\second}\). In particular,
\(|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{22}|/|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{11}|\) decreases from
about \(0.41\) at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{16}{\femto\second}\) to \(0.10\) at
\(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{140}{\femto\second}\). At the same time,
\(|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{21}|/|\Re\,\tilde{E}_{22}|\) rises above
unity by \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{140}{\femto\second}\) and remains above unity
at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{310}{\femto\second}\). This late-time trend is
consistent with Redfield population transfer from the upper to the lower
one-exciton eigenstate; at finite temperature, the final population ratio is
set by the Boltzmann factor.

The nonrephasing branch is shown in~\autoref{fig:rslts_dimer_fig4_waiting_time_nonrephasing}. It is also informative because it distributes the waiting-time modulation differently over the four peak positions.

The rephasing and nonrephasing branches show different waiting-time dependences. In the rephasing signal, the short-time oscillation is clearest in the cross peaks, while the diagonal peaks remain nearly constant. This agrees with the pathway-level expectation that oscillatory cross-peak contributions are mainly associated with rephasing pathways~\cite{mancal2022nonlinearopticalspectroscopy}. In the nonrephasing signal, the modulation is less isolated. The sequence alternates between panels dominated by off-diagonal peaks at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{16}{\femto\second}\) and \(\SI{46}{\femto\second}\), and panels dominated by diagonal peaks at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{30}{\femto\second}\) and \(\SI{62}{\femto\second}\).

\subsection{Dimer Summary}
\label{subsec:results_dimer_summary}

The dimer results extend the monomer benchmark from broadening physics to coupled excitonic dynamics. At zero waiting time, the coupled system shows cross peaks in addition to the diagonal peaks. Their presence identifies excitonic coupling between the one-exciton states, whereas the uncoupled dimer shows only the diagonal-peak structure.

Beyond the pulse-overlap regime, the spectra vary with waiting time. For the present parameter set, the modulation follows the exciton splitting \(\omega_{21}\). It is therefore consistent with an electronic coherence between the exciton states and appears as a periodic redistribution of spectral weight across diagonal and off-diagonal peaks.

At longer waiting times, the oscillation is damped and the spectra show an additional intensity redistribution. This slower change is consistent with Redfield population transfer within the one-exciton manifold. As expected from the monomer case, the homogeneous companion keeps the same dimer peak positions but shows narrower features.

Overall, the dimer results show three physical signatures that are absent in the monomer: cross peaks as a signature of excitonic coupling, waiting-time beats as a signature of electronic coherence, and late-time intensity redistribution as a signature of dissipative population dynamics.


\section{Selected Results for Larger \texorpdfstring{$N$}{N}-Site Models}
\label{sec:results_nsite_extension}

The same procedure is now applied to larger models with \(N\) sites, defined in~\autoref{sec:model_ensemble}. This section presents selected ring-aggregate examples. Their role is illustrative: they show how the same signal construction can also be evaluated for more complex systems.

\subsection{Setup for the Present Figures}
\label{subsec:results_nsite_model}

All extension figures in this section use homogeneous ring aggregates with \(\Delta_{\mathrm{inh}}=0\), so no disorder averaging is required. The site dipoles are aligned and of equal strength, \(\mu_n=1\). The field is modelled by three Gaussian pulses with \(\tau_{\mathrm{p}}=\SI{12}{\femto\second}\) FWHM, amplitudes \((0.002,0.002,0.002)\), carrier frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{L}}=\SI{16000}{\per\centi\metre}\), and the rotating-wave approximation. All four ring-aggregate cases are treated with a Redfield description with an Ohmic bath at \(T=\SI{77}{\kelvin}\) and \(s=1\). The simulated time windows are \(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{det}}\in[0,\SI{600}{\femto\second}]\), with \(\Delta t=\SI{2}{\femto\second}\).

The case-specific parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Trimer \(1\mathrm{ex}\).} Site energies \(15600\), \(16000\), and \(16400~\si{\per\centi\metre}\), nearest-neighbour ring coupling \(J=\SI{180}{\per\centi\metre}\), bath cutoff \(\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=0.66\bar{\omega}_0\), system-bath coupling \(0.026\), and \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{50}{\femto\second}\).
\item \textbf{Trimer \(2\mathrm{ex}\).} Same parameters as the trimer with \(1\mathrm{ex}\), but with excitations included up to the double-excitation manifold.
\item \textbf{Pentamer \(1\mathrm{ex}\).} Site energies \(15600\), \(15800\), \(16000\), \(16200\), and \(16400~\si{\per\centi\metre}\), \(J=\SI{135}{\per\centi\metre}\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=2.0\bar{\omega}_0\), system-bath coupling \(0.0261\bar{\omega}_0\), and \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{50}{\femto\second}\).
\item \textbf{Tridecamer \(1\mathrm{ex}\).} \(N=13\), site energies \(16350\), \(16275\), \(16120\), \(15945\), \(15825\), \(15735\), \(15712\), \(15720\), \(15755\), \(15865\), \(16040\), \(16225\), and \(16385~\si{\per\centi\metre}\), \(J=\SI{85}{\per\centi\metre}\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=0.75\bar{\omega}_0\), system-bath coupling \(0.022\bar{\omega}_0\), full non-secular Redfield propagation, and \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}\).
\end{itemize}

The examples in~\cref{fig:rslts_extension_ring_time_overview,fig:rslts_extension_ring_freq_overview} compare four ring-aggregate cases: a trimer ring with only the single-excitation manifold included, a trimer ring with the single- and double-excitation manifolds included, a pentamer ring with only the single-excitation manifold included, and a tridecamer ring with only the single-excitation manifold included. In the figure labels, \(1\mathrm{ex}\) denotes a model restricted to the single-excitation manifold, whereas \(2\mathrm{ex}\) denotes a model that also includes the double-excitation manifold.

These four cases are single waiting-time snapshots rather than waiting-time series. The trimer and pentamer panels are evaluated at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{50}{\femto\second}\), while the tridecamer panel is evaluated at \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}=\SI{0}{\femto\second}\). They should therefore be read as structural snapshots of the larger-system response rather than as waiting-time dynamics. Because the waiting time, bath parameters, and manifold truncation are not identical across the four ring-aggregate cases, the panels are compared only at the level of visible spectral structure. As discussed for the monomer and dimer, the zero-waiting-time case can contain finite-pulse-overlap contributions.

The trimer \(1\mathrm{ex}\) case in panel~(a) shows a regular stripe pattern in both the rephasing and nonrephasing rows. The absolute-value plots are strongest near short \(t_{\mathrm{coh}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{det}}\) and become weaker towards longer times.

In panel~(b), where the double-excitation manifold is included, the real and imaginary parts still show stripe-like oscillations. \section{Summary and Outlook}
\label{sec:results_summary_outlook}

The results in this chapter follow the hierarchy introduced in~\autoref{chapter:model_systems}: the monomer isolates the effect of homogeneous broadening and static disorder. In this limit, the time-domain echo appears only when static disorder is included, and the spectra reproduce the expected distinction between a homogeneous line shape and an inhomogeneously broadened photon echo, with the corresponding 2D spectrum elongated along the diagonal.

The dimer then adds coherent coupling, a double-excitation state, and waiting-time dynamics. All these results are simulated with a microscopic Redfield model. The coupled spectra contain cross peaks that are absent in the uncoupled dimer. The waiting-time sequence provides additional information: the cross-peak amplitudes oscillate on the timescale set by the exciton splitting, while the longer-time changes are consistent with dissipative population transfer within the one-exciton manifold.

The selected larger examples extend the thesis to ring aggregates with \(N\ge 3\). They show that the same pulse-propagation, phase-cycling, and Fourier-analysis chain can also be evaluated for larger aggregates, where the displayed spectra are denser.

Several limitations should be kept explicit. The monomer and dimer benchmarks are not exact reproductions of the reference figures. The remaining differences may be related to pulse-overlap effects, finite time windows, or finite disorder sampling. In the dimer spectra, the relative size of some features is also smaller than in the reference calculation, although the same parameters were used. Nevertheless, the main visual trends are reproduced: static disorder produces the photon-echo shape in the monomer, and excitonic coupling produces cross peaks in the dimer.

Future work could therefore analyse geometry-dependent spectra for larger systems with \(N\ge 3\). Such a study could then compare different aggregate sizes, geometries, coupling patterns, and site-energy arrangements.

The present work used an Ohmic bath with an exponential cutoff. The same spectroscopy workflow could also be combined with other spectral densities. Structured baths, for example, could be used to study how discrete vibrational modes appear in the two-dimensional spectra. Another useful extension would be a direct comparison between secular and non-secular Redfield propagation for the Redfield-based models studied here.

Beyond the Redfield regime, the same signal construction could be coupled within the present workflow to a non-perturbative propagation method, such as the hierarchical equations of motion (HEOM)~\cite{lambertetal2023qutipbofinbosonicfermionic}. This is a natural next step towards studying stronger system-environment coupling and more structured baths in the same spectroscopic framework.
