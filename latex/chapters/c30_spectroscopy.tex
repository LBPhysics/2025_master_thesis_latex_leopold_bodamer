\section{Motivation and Historical Development}
\label{sec:spectroscopy_motivation}


Spectroscopy, in the broadest sense, studies how matter interacts with energy---most commonly through absorption, emission, or scattering of radiation \cite{skoogetal2017principlesinstrumentalanalysis}. Atoms and molecules occupy discrete energy levels. Therefore, the frequency of light absorbed or emitted during a transition satisfies the Planck relation $\Delta E=h\nu=hc/\lambda$. Early optical spectroscopy exploited this fact. The stationary line spectra of atoms provided the first ``fingerprints'' of quantum energy levels \cite{balmer1885notizuberspectrallinien, rydberg1890xxxivstructurelinespectra, bohr1913constitutionatomsmolecules}. Quantum mechanics later related these to transitions between eigenstates of the microscopic Hamiltonian \cite{berman2011principleslaserspectroscopy, dirac1927quantumtheoryemission}. In practice, transitions are labeled by the wavenumber $\tilde\nu=1/\lambda\;[\mathrm{cm}^{-1}]$, which is directly proportional to energy. The electronic transitions studied in this thesis lie in the visible and near-UV range ($\tilde\nu\sim 13\,000$--$25\,000\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$). In this regime, the relevant coherence and relaxation dynamics unfold on femtosecond timescales.
%TODO this was removed by AI: Spectroscopy can be categorized by the nature of the transition (rotational, vibrational, electronic) and the region of the electromagnetic spectrum probed: rotational transitions lie in the microwave region and reveal molecular geometry; vibrational spectroscopy probes bonding patterns in the infrared ($\tilde{\nu}\sim 400$--$4000\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$); and electronic transitions, the focus of this thesis, occur in the visible and UV range ($\tilde{\nu}\sim 13{,}000$--$25{,}000\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$), enabling studies of excited-state structure and dynamics. This energetic hierarchy reflects timescales: electronic coherences and transitions occur on femtosecond timescales, vibrational relaxation on picoseconds, and chemical reactions on micro- to millisecond scales.
%TODO this was removed by AI: With advancing experimental resolution, subtle effects such as fine and hyperfine structure and, later, the Lamb shift became accessible~\cite{michelsonmorley1887lixmethodmaking, rabietal1938newmethodmeasuring, lambretherford1947finestructurehydrogen}, providing precise benchmarks that confirmed the predictions of quantum electrodynamics and strengthened the link between atomic spectra and fundamental theory.

For decades, spectroscopy was essentially \emph{linear}: a weak field probes the sample and one records intensity as a function of frequency. Linear absorption and emission reveal excitation energies, oscillator strengths, and broadening mechanisms. However, they compress dynamical information---such as coherence loss, population transfer, and spectral diffusion---into one-dimensional lineshapes. As a result, different microscopic mechanisms are difficult to disentangle.

The development of lasers in the 1960s made it possible to generate intense, coherent light fields. This opened the door to \emph{nonlinear} optics. The induced polarization $\boldsymbol{P}(t)$ contains not only components linear in the applied field $\boldsymbol{E}(t)$~\cite{kubo1957statisticalmechanicaltheoryirreversible},
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}(t) = \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\;
\boldsymbol{S}^{(1)}(\tau)\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}(t-\tau),
\end{equation*}
where $\boldsymbol{S}^{(1)}$ is the causal linear-response kernel. There are also quadratic, cubic, and higher-order contributions,
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t) \propto \boldsymbol{E}(t)^n,\qquad n\ge 2.
\end{equation*}
These additional terms are responsible for phenomena such as second-harmonic generation, four-wave mixing, and stimulated Raman scattering. Conceptually, they probe \emph{multi-time} correlation functions of the system. Therefore, they encode dynamical information that is inaccessible in the linear-response regime~\cite{frankenetal1961generationopticalharmonics, mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical,hamm2005principlesnonlinearoptical}.

Two-dimensional Fourier-transform spectroscopy represents a powerful experimental tool in nonlinear spectroscopy. By correlating excitation and detection frequencies, it separates homogeneous and inhomogeneous contributions to spectral line shapes. Inhomogeneous broadening arises from static disorder in the molecular environment (e.g.\ different binding sites or solvent configurations), which distributes the transition frequencies across the ensemble. Homogeneous broadening, in contrast, reflects the intrinsic linewidth of a single chromophore, determined by coherence loss and population relaxation. Two-dimensional spectra reveal the homogeneous linewidth even in strongly inhomogeneously broadened systems~\cite{bangertetal2022highresolutiontwodimensionalelectronic, freschetal2023twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}. Additionally, cross peaks directly visualize couplings between transitions and energy-transfer pathways.

To connect these experimental capabilities to the theoretical framework developed in~\autoref{chapter_open_quantum_systems}, the response-function formalism is employed. It systematically links the applied pulse sequence to the measured signal via the induced polarization. Response functions are multi-time dipole correlation functions evaluated with respect to the equilibrium density matrix. By separating matter contributions (how incident fields generate $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}$, derived in~\autoref{app:response_functions}) from field contributions (how $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ radiates the signal, derived in~\autoref{app:2dfts_maxwell}), this formalism makes the perturbative order explicit, naturally incorporates phase-matching conditions, and interfaces with open-quantum-system master equations such as the Redfield equation (see~\autoref{eq:Redfield_Markov_TimeLocal} microscopically derived in~\autoref{sec:Redfield_eq}).

The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows: \autoref{sec:light_matter} introduces the semiclassical light--matter Hamiltonian in the dipole approximation; \autoref{sec:pol_and_macr_response} defines the macroscopic polarization and its role in the signal (field-side details in~\autoref{app:2dfts_maxwell}); \autoref{sec:response_formalism} states the response-function results used later (matter-side derivations in~\autoref{app:response_functions}); and \autoref{sec:three_pulse_geometry}--\ref{sec:2D_construction_formal} applies the formalism to three-pulse experiments, phase matching, phase cycling, and 2D spectrum construction.

\section{Light--Matter Interaction in the Dipole Approximation}
\label{sec:light_matter}

Throughout this chapter, a semiclassical description of light is adopted. 
The electromagnetic field is treated as a classical, time-dependent driving field, 
while the matter degrees of freedom are described quantum mechanically. 
The central object is the density operator of the matter, whose evolution determines the optical response.

The microscopic dynamics of the full (molecule + environment) system are governed by the Liouville--von Neumann equation (cf.~\autoref{eq:Von_Neumann_Equation}) 
\begin{equation}
	\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}_T(t) = -i\,[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}_T(t)],
	\label{eq:LvN_total_spectroscopy}
\end{equation}
with total Hamiltonian
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_S(t)+\hat{H}_E+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}.
\end{equation*}
Here $\hat{H}_E$ is the environment Hamiltonian and $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}$ the system--environment coupling. 
The semiclassical light field enters exclusively through the time-dependent system Hamiltonian $\hat{H}_S(t)$. The structure therefore is a time-dependent version of~\autoref{sec:setup_system_environment}.

\subsection{Minimal coupling and dipole approximation}

Starting from the minimal-coupling Hamiltonian for a particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ in the Coulomb gauge,
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}, t) = \frac{1}{2m}\Bigl[\hat{\boldsymbol{p}} + q\,\boldsymbol{A}(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},t)\Bigr]^2
	+ V(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}) - q\,\phi(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},t),
\end{equation*}
where $\boldsymbol{A}(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},t)$ is the classical vector potential, $\phi(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},t)$ the scalar potential, and $V(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}})$ the static binding potential of the particle. In the long-wavelength (electric-dipole) approximation for neutral optical transitions, the familiar length-gauge interaction is obtained~\cite{cohen-tannoudjietal2008atomphotoninteractionsbasic,berman2011principleslaserspectroscopy}, $\hat{H}_S(t)=\hat{H}_0 - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}(t)$,
where $\hat{H}_0$ is the field-free system Hamiltonian and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ the electric dipole operator. 
The electric field is assumed to vary negligibly over the molecular dimensions.
For notational simplicity, a fixed linear polarization direction $\boldsymbol{e}$ is chosen. 
Defining $E(t)=\boldsymbol{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}(t)$ and $\hat{\mu}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}$, the interaction reduces to
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{H}_S(t)=\hat{H}_0 - \hat{\mu}\,E(t).
\end{equation*}
This corresponds to choosing the field polarization
parallel to the relevant dipole component.

\medskip
A key simplification in the subsequent treatment is the \emph{rotating-wave approximation} (RWA). For narrow-linewidth optical transitions driven near resonance, the real field is decomposed into positive- and negative-frequency components $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ and $\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$, and only the near-resonant cross terms $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(+)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(-)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ are retained. Rapid oscillations at twice the optical frequency are thereby discarded, clarifying the microscopic mechanisms and enabling a clean separation of rephasing and nonrephasing contributions in multidimensional spectra. The RWA is developed in detail below (see particularly \autoref{subsec:phase_kick_pulse_area_intuition} and \autoref{subsec:rephasing_nonrephasing}).

\section{Polarization and macroscopic response}
\label{sec:pol_and_macr_response}

Maxwell’s equations show that the emitted signal field is sourced by the macroscopic polarization 
$\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r},t)$ of the medium. This is derived in~\autoref{app:2dfts_maxwell}.

For convenience, the key steps are summarized here:
In Maxwell’s equations, $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r},t)$ acts as a source term,
\begin{equation*}
	\nabla \times \nabla \times \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)
	+ \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}
	\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)
	= - \mu_0 \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}
	\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r}, t).
\end{equation*}
In the perturbative regime, the polarization can be expanded in a power series
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{P}
	=\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}
	+\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}+\cdots,
\end{equation*}
and in four-wave mixing the detected signal can be obtained via phase matching. The heterodyne cross term makes the detected signal linear in the emitted field
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{E}_S^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	\propto
	i\,\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t).
\end{equation*}
The response-function formalism then provides $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ as a causal convolution of the intrinsic matter response $S^{(3)}$ with the incident fields (\autoref{app:response_functions}).

The microscopic task is therefore to compute the expectation value of the dipole operator.
For a single molecule coupled to an environment,
\begin{equation*}
	\langle \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t) \rangle
	= \mathrm{Tr}_{SE}\!\left\{
	\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\hat{\rho}_T(t)
	\right\}.
\end{equation*}
If the dipole operator acts only on system degrees of freedom, tracing out the environment yields
\begin{equation*}
	\langle \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t) \rangle
	= \mathrm{Tr}_S\!\left\{
	\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\hat{\rho}_S(t)
	\right\},
	\qquad
	\hat{\rho}_S(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_E\,\hat{\rho}_T(t).
\end{equation*}
The total density operator $\hat{\rho}_T(t)$ evolves unitarily according to \autoref{eq:LvN_total_spectroscopy}. 
By contrast, the reduced density operator $\hat{\rho}_S(t)$ does not, in general, satisfy a unitary equation of motion. 
Eliminating the bath degrees of freedom leads to an effective master equation
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}_S(t)=\mathcal{L}(t)\hat{\rho}_S(t),
\end{equation*}
whose derivation and approximations (Born--Markov, secular vs.\ non-secular) are discussed in 
\autoref{chapter_open_quantum_systems}. 
In the following, dissipative effects such as population relaxation and dephasing enter exclusively through this reduced dynamics.

For a representative molecule, the microscopic polarization is therefore defined as
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{P}(t)
	= \mathrm{Tr}_S\!\bigl[
	\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\hat{\rho}_S(t)
	\bigr].
	\label{eq:P_def_spectroscopy}
\end{equation}
In a chosen basis $\{\ket{n}\}$ of the system Hilbert space this becomes 
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{P}(t)=\sum_{n,m}
	\boldsymbol{\mu}_{nm}\,\rho_{S,mn}(t),
	\qquad
	\boldsymbol{\mu}_{nm}=\bra{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\ket{m}.
\end{equation*}
Oscillating polarization is generated by optical coherences, i.e.\ off-diagonal elements of the density matrix. If the relevant electronic states carry no permanent dipoles in the chosen eigenbasis, i.e.\ $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{nn}=\boldsymbol{0}$, then populations do not contribute.

For an ensemble of identical, noninteracting molecules with number density $N(\mathbf{r})$,
\begin{equation*}
	\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r},t)
	\simeq N(\mathbf{r})
	\big\langle
	\boldsymbol{P}(t)
	\big\rangle_{\mathrm{or}},
\end{equation*}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{or}}$ denotes orientational averaging.



\section{Response Function Formalism}
\label{sec:response_formalism}

The response-function formalism connects the applied field $\boldsymbol{E}(t)$ to the induced polarization $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t)$. The derivations are in~\autoref{app:response_functions}; here the main results are stated.
Linear response is the simplest setting in which (i) the causal time-domain structure of the optical response becomes transparent and (ii) The structure of liouvillian pathways emerges, which translates into the diagrammatic time domain language of spectroscopy. For these reasons, the linear response is discussed in great detail.


\subsection{Linear Response and the Kubo Formula}
\label{sec:linear_response}

Specializing \autoref{eq:app_Pn_convolution} and \autoref{eq:app_Sn_def} to $n=1$ gives
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}(t)
= \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1\,\boldsymbol{S}^{(1)}(\tau_1)\,E(t-\tau_1),
\label{eq:P1_convolution_thesis}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{S}^{(1)}(\tau_1)
= \mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\bigr\}.
\label{eq:S1_def_superop_thesis}
\end{equation}
Importantly, the linear response is tensorial and anisotropic \cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}. Writing out the commutator structure in time-domain components, one finds
\begin{equation*}
	S^{(1)}_{ij}(\tau)
	= i\,\Theta(\tau)\,\big\langle\big[\hat{\mu}_{i,I}(\tau),\hat{\mu}_{j,I}(0)\big]\big\rangle_{\mathrm{eq}}.
\end{equation*}


\subsubsection{Diagrammatic interpretation}
\label{sec:linear_dsfd}

Even at first order, \autoref{eq:S1_def_superop_thesis} decomposes into two Liouville-space pathways. For $\tau>0$:
\begin{equation}
	J_{1,ij}(\tau) \equiv \mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{i,I}(\tau)\,\hat{\mu}_{j,I}(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\big\},
	\qquad
	J_{1,ij}^*(\tau) \equiv \mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{i,I}(\tau)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,I}(0)\big\},
	\label{eq:J1_definitions}
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
	S^{(1)}_{ij}(\tau)
	= i\bigl[J_{1,ij}(\tau)-J_{1,ij}^*(\tau)\bigr],
	\qquad \tau>0.
	\label{eq:S1_J1_scalar_thesis}
\end{equation}
The two contributions correspond to whether the interaction acts on the ket or bra with a relative minus sign.

To make the structure of the linear response more transparent, we now consider the simplest example, namely a two-level system. 
This allows us to evaluate the general expressions explicitly and to introduce the double-sided Feynman representation, which will later serve as a natural language for discussing higher-order processes (see \autoref{sec:third_order_response}).

\subsubsection{Two-level system}
\label{sec:two_level_system_linear_response}
For optical transitions ($\tilde{\nu}_0 \approx 16\,000\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$), $\omega_0/(k_BT)\approx 77$ at room temperature gives $p_1/p_0\approx 10^{-34}$. Initial condition: electronic ground state. 

For a two-level system with $\ket{g}$ and $\ket{e}$ the equilibrium state is written as
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}} = \ket{g}\bra{g}.
\end{equation*}
and the dipole operator is written in terms of the transition dipole moment $d$ and the dimensionless operator $\hat{m}$ as
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{\mu}=d\,\hat{m} \quad\text{with}\quad
	\hat{m} = \ket{e}\bra{g} + \ket{g}\bra{e}.
\end{equation*}
The two simplest pathways \autoref{eq:J1_definitions} from the first order response function \autoref{eq:S1_J1_scalar_thesis} are visualized as double-sided Feynman diagrams in~\autoref{fig:linear_response_diagrams}.

\subsubsection{Frequency-domain susceptibility and absorption}


The linear susceptibility is the Fourier transform of the causal response kernel,
\begin{equation*}
	\chi^{(1)}(\omega)
	= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,S^{(1)}(\tau)
	\mathrm{e}^{i\omega\tau}
	= \int_0^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,S^{(1)}(\tau)
	\mathrm{e}^{i\omega\tau},
\end{equation*}
where $S^{(1)}(\tau<0)=0$ has been used. The linear polarization in frequency space then becomes
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}(\omega) = \chi^{(1)}(\omega)\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}(\omega).
\end{equation*}

In terms of the level pathways $J_1(\tau)$ and $J_1^*(\tau)$ defined in~\autoref{eq:S1_J1_scalar_thesis}, this may be written explicitly as
\begin{equation*}
	\chi^{(1)}(\omega)
	= i\,d^2
	\int_0^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,
	\bigl[J_1(\tau)-J_1^*(\tau)\bigr] \
	\mathrm{e}^{i\omega\tau}.
\end{equation*}

To connect to conventional lineshapes, the pathway is approximated by the coherence propagator, $J_1(\tau)\propto G_{eg}(\tau)$ with $G_{eg}(\tau)=\exp[-(i\omega_{eg}+\Gamma_{eg})\tau]$, where $\omega_{eg}=E_e-E_g$ and $\Gamma_{eg}$ is the optical dephasing rate.
. This yields
\begin{equation*}
	\int_0^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,
	G_{eg}(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\omega\tau}
	= \int_0^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,
	\exp\bigl[-(\Gamma_{eg}+i(\omega_{eg}-\omega))\tau\bigr]
	= \frac{1}{\Gamma_{eg}+i(\omega_{eg}-\omega)},
\end{equation*}
and therefore the familiar complex Lorentzian susceptibility
\begin{equation*}
	\chi^{(1)}(\omega)
	\propto d^2
	\frac{1}{\Gamma_{eg} - i(\omega-\omega_{eg})},
\end{equation*}
whose imaginary part gives the absorptive Lorentzian line shape centered at $\omega_{eg}$ with half-width $\Gamma_{eg}$. The absorption coefficient is $\alpha(\omega)\propto \operatorname{Im}\chi^{(1)}(\omega)$. For multilevel systems the total absorption is a sum over all allowed $g\to e$ transitions.

More generally, the cumulant-expansion lineshape theory expresses the absorption as~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}
\begin{equation*}
	I(\omega) \propto \Re\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t\;
	\exp\Bigl\{ i(\omega-\omega_{eg})t - g(t) - \Gamma t \Bigr\},
\end{equation*}
where $g(t)$ is the lineshape function determined by the environment correlation function (\autoref{chapter_open_quantum_systems}). The simple Lorentzian limit corresponds to $g(t)=0$.


\subsection{Nonlinear Response} % Third-Order Response
\label{sec:third_order_response}


At high field intensities, the response of the medium becomes nonlinear. In general, the macroscopic polarization can be expanded as a power series in the incident electric field. In explicit Cartesian-index form,
\begin{equation*}
	P_i
	=
	\varepsilon_0\left(\chi^{(1)}_{ij}E_j + \chi^{(2)}_{ijk}E_jE_k + \chi^{(3)}_{ijkl}E_jE_kE_l + \cdots\right),
\end{equation*}
with implicit summation over repeated indices, where $\varepsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity and $\chi^{(n)}$ denotes the $n$-th-order susceptibility tensor.
In the linear regime, the induced polarization is directly proportional to the applied field ($\chi^{(1)}$), describing refraction and absorption. Higher-order terms give rise to a variety of nonlinear optical phenomena~\cite{boyd2008chapter6nonlinear}.


Since even-order susceptibilities vanish in centrosymmetric media, the lowest-order nonlinear response relevant here is third order ($\chi^{(3)}$)~\cite{hamm2005principlesnonlinearoptical}. The most important $\chi^{(3)}$ process for this thesis is \textbf{four-wave mixing (FWM)}~\cite{boyd2008chapter6nonlinear}, in which three incident fields generate a signal field. The resulting photon-echo and free-induction-decay contributions are the basis of 2D spectroscopy.

\medskip

The response-function framework separates intrinsic matter response from field ordering and connects microscopic dynamics to experimentally selected signals via phase matching or phase cycling. The matter-side derivation is in~\autoref{app:response_functions}; here we only quote the third-order results used later. The tensor form is in~\autoref{app:sec:tensorial_structure}, and we work with a fixed polarization (scalar component).

\subsubsection{Third-order response function \texorpdfstring{$S^{(3)}$}{S(3)}}


The third-order polarization is of central interest for four-wave-mixing and 2D spectroscopy. From~\autoref{app:response_functions}, the standard causal convolution and response function are
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:P3_convolution_thesis}
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(t)
	= \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_3\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_2\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1\,\boldsymbol{S}^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
	\,E(t-\tau_3)\,E(t-\tau_3-\tau_2)\,E(t-\tau_3-\tau_2-\tau_1),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:S3_def_thesis}
	\boldsymbol{S}^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
	= (\mathrm{i})^3\,\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_3)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_2)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\Bigr\}.
\end{equation}

This third-order response contains the multi-time correlations central to the present thesis. In a three-pulse experiment one writes $E(t)=E_1(t)+E_2(t)+E_3(t)$; inserting this into \autoref{eq:P3_convolution_thesis} generates many terms, and the experimentally relevant contribution (e.g. rephasing) is selected by phase matching or phase cycling. The material kernel responsible for that selection is precisely \autoref{eq:S3_def_thesis}~\cite{hammzanni2011conceptsmethods2d}.

\subsubsection{Frequency-domain bookkeeping}

After decomposing each pulse into positive- and negative-frequency parts, the time-domain convolution maps to frequency mixing governed by $\chi^{(3)}$. A compact bookkeeping form is
\begin{equation}
	P_i^{(3)}(t)
	=
	\varepsilon_0
	\sum_{jkl}
	\sum_{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3=\pm 1}
	\chi^{(3)}_{ijkl}\big(-\omega_S;\sigma_1\omega_1,\sigma_2\omega_2,\sigma_3\omega_3\big)
	\,E_{1,j}^{(\sigma_1)}E_{2,k}^{(\sigma_2)}E_{3,l}^{(\sigma_3)}\,e^{-i\omega_S t},
	\label{eq:chi3_frequency_bookkeeping_thesis}
\end{equation}
with $\omega_S=\sigma_1\omega_1+\sigma_2\omega_2+\sigma_3\omega_3$. Fixing the field polarization directions reduces this tensorial expression to the scalar forms used in the numerical and analytical sections below.



\subsubsection{Time-domain picture and impulsive limit}


The convolution form \autoref{eq:P3_convolution_thesis} makes the \emph{causal} time ordering explicit. For fixed delays $(\tau_1,\tau_2,\tau_3)$, the three interactions with the field occur at the times
\begin{equation*}
	t-\tau_3,\qquad t-\tau_3-\tau_2,\qquad t-\tau_3-\tau_2-\tau_1,
\end{equation*}
and the system evolves freely for durations $\tau_1$, $\tau_2$, and $\tau_3$ between these events. Reading the operator product in~\autoref{eq:S3_def_thesis} from right to left corresponds precisely to this physical sequence: start from $\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}$, apply a dipole interaction, evolve freely, apply another interaction, and so on, followed by the dipole readout at time $t$.

\medskip

The physical interpretation of this causal ordering, and the impulsive-limit simplification for ultrashort pulses, was already discussed when relating the detected signal field to $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ in~\autoref{sec:light_matter}. This section is therefore kept brief and the above expressions are used mainly as the starting point for pathway decompositions below.

In experiments with finite pulse durations or partial pulse overlap, strict time ordering is blurred and the signal becomes a weighted convolution with the pulse envelopes. This can redistribute pathway amplitudes\todoref{find a source? is it true?}. In the nonperturbative simulations used here, these effects are naturally included.

\subsubsection{Again:Two-level system}
\label{sec:two_level_third_order_pathways}

To see concretely how the compact operator expression expands into distinct contributions, consider again the two-level system from \autoref{sec:two_level_system_linear_response}. Now define the commutator superoperator $\mathcal{M}[\hat{A}]\equiv[\hat{m},\hat{A}]$. Then the  $\mathcal{V}=d\,\mathcal{M}$ and the third-order response can be written in the same structural form as~\autoref{eq:S3_def_thesis}:
\begin{equation}
    S^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
    \propto \mathrm{Tr}\Bigl\{\hat{m}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_3)\,\mathcal{M}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_2)\,\mathcal{M}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{M}[\ket{g}\!\bra{g}]\Bigr\}.
	\label{eq:S3_M_commutator_not_explicit}
\end{equation}
By expanding the first commutator, the initial population operator $\ket{g}\!\bra{g}$ is converted into a superposition of optical coherences,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{M}[\ket{g}\!\bra{g}]
    = \ket{e}\!\bra{g} - \ket{g}\!\bra{e}.
\end{equation*}
The two terms already illustrate the pathway logic: each commutator produces a \emph{left} and a \emph{right} action (ket vs.\ bra), with a relative minus sign. During the first delay $\tau_1$, these coherences undergo free evolution. In the secular approximation (coherences do not mix), the coherences evolve independently with the coherence Green function $G_{eg}(\tau)=\exp[-(i\omega_{eg}+\Gamma_{eg})\tau]$:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)[\ket{e}\!\bra{g}]\approx G_{eg}(\tau_1)\,\ket{e}\!\bra{g},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)[\ket{g}\!\bra{e}]\approx G_{eg}^*(\tau_1)\,\ket{g}\!\bra{e}.
\end{equation*}
A second light--matter interaction is described by applying $\mathcal{M}$ again, which converts these coherences into population operators,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{M}[\ket{e}\!\bra{g}] = \ket{g}\!\bra{g} - \ket{e}\!\bra{e},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{M}[\ket{g}\!\bra{e}] = \ket{e}\!\bra{e} - \ket{g}\!\bra{g},
\end{equation*}
with optical phase factors carried by $G_{eg}(\tau_1)$ and $G_{eg}^*(\tau_1)$. During $\tau_2$ the density operator is (in this simple model) a linear combination of populations. The third commutator converts populations back into coherences, which evolve during $\tau_3$ and are finally converted into a measurable polarization by the left multiplication with $\hat{m}$ and the trace.

Writing the nested commutator \autoref{eq:S3_M_commutator_not_explicit} explicitly
\begin{equation*}
	S^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
	=(-i)
	\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,[\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}),[\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1),[\hat{\mu}_I(0),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]]]\Big\},
\end{equation*}
one obtains the $2^3=8$ operator products
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S4}
	S^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1) & = \mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_4
	\\
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S1star}
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_1^{*}
	\\
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S2star}
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_2^{*}
	\\
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S3}
	                              & +\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_3
	\\
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S3star}
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_3^{*}
	\\
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S2}
	                              & +\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_2
	\\
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S1}
	                              & +\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_1
	\\
	\label{eq:R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S4star}
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_I(0)\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_I(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad S_4^{*}.
\end{align}
Along the same lines as in linear response (see \autoref{eq:S1_J1_scalar_thesis}), the terms labeled $S_\alpha^{*}$ are the complex conjugates of $S_\alpha$ for Hermitian $\hat{\mu}$ and a real equilibrium state. In typical 2DES calculations (RWA) one therefore groups the response as
\begin{equation*}
	S^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
	= (-i) \sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}\Big[ S_{\alpha}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1) - S_{\alpha}^{*}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1) \Big].
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Liouville-space pathways and double-sided Feynman diagrams}
\label{sec:dsfd}


The superoperator representation ( makes the origin of \emph{pathways} transparent. Because $\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{V}_L-\mathcal{V}_R)$, the product $\mathcal{V}_{j_3}\mathcal{V}_{j_2}\mathcal{V}_{j_1}$ expands into a sum of terms with different choices of left/right actions at the three interaction times.
Each such term corresponds to a distinct time-ordered evolution of the density matrix elements and is conveniently visualized by a \emph{double-sided Feynman diagram} (also called a Liouville-space pathway diagram).

The basic rules are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Two vertical lines represent the ket (left) and bra (right) branches of $\hat{\rho}_S$.
	\item Time flows upward. The three field interactions occur at $\tau_1$, $\tau_1{+}\tau_2$, and $\tau_1{+}\tau_2{+}\tau_3$.
	\item An interaction acting on the ket contributes $+1$ to the global sign. An interaction on the bra contributes $-1$. This sign structure follows from the commutator decomposition $\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{V}_L - \mathcal{V}_R)$ inherited from the nested commutators in the response function.
	\item Under the rotating-wave approximation one further assigns whether the interaction involves $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ or $\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ (absorption vs.\ emission). This fixes which optical coherence is created and thus determines the overall phase factor and the phase-matching direction.
\end{itemize}

For interpretation, the eight third-order pathways are regrouped into three physical classes: ground-state bleach (GSB), stimulated emission (SE), and excited-state absorption (ESA). This classification is standard in 2DES and will be used when discussing peak assignments~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy}.

\subsubsection{Relaxation during the waiting time}
\label{sec:two_level_relaxation_channel}


During the waiting interval $T$, population relaxation modifies the third-order pathways. For a two-level system, the relevant dissipative channel is spontaneous decay $\ket{e}\to\ket{g}$ at rate $k_{ge}$, described by the Lindblad jump operator $\hat{L}=\sqrt{k_{ge}}\,\ket{g}\!\bra{e}$ (see\ \autoref{chapter_open_quantum_systems}). The resulting equations of motion for the density-matrix elements are
\begin{align}
	\dot{\rho}_{ee} &= -k_{ge}\,\rho_{ee}, &
	\dot{\rho}_{gg} &= +k_{ge}\,\rho_{ee}, &
	\dot{\rho}_{eg} &= -(k_{ge}/2 + \gamma_\varphi)\,\rho_{eg},
\end{align}
where $\gamma_\varphi$ accounts for additional pure dephasing. Solving these gives the population propagators
\begin{equation}
	U_{ee,ee}(T) = \mathrm{e}^{-k_{ge}T},\qquad
	U_{gg,ee}(T) = 1-\mathrm{e}^{-k_{ge}T},\qquad
	U_{gg,gg}(T) = 1,
	\label{eq:population_propagators_two_level_thesis}
\end{equation}
and the total dephasing rate of the optical coherence is $\Gamma_{eg}=k_{ge}/2+\gamma_{\varphi}$, with $T_1=1/k_{ge}$ and $T_2=1/\Gamma_{eg}$.

Consequently, every pathway that passes through $\rho_{ee}$ during $T$ splits into a contribution that remains in $ee$ (multiplied by $U_{ee,ee}$, yielding standard SE/ESA signals that decay with the lifetime) and a contribution that has relaxed to $gg$ (multiplied by $U_{gg,ee}$, a relaxation-induced pathway acting on the refilled ground state). In the numerical simulations used later, this splitting is generated automatically by the Liouvillian propagator.

\subsubsection{Multilevel generalization}
\label{sec:multilevel_generalization_thesis}


Realistic systems typically have many optically accessible excited states, and a central goal of this thesis is to extend the simulation framework from a minimal two-level model to multilevel Hamiltonians. A convenient (and widely used) idealisation that already captures the essential physics of ground-state bleach (GSB), stimulated emission (SE), and excited-state absorption (ESA) is a three-manifold ``band'' picture:
\begin{itemize}
	\item a single ground state $\ket{g}$.
	\item a manifold of singly excited states $\{\ket{n}\}$.
	\item a manifold of higher excited (doubly excited) states $\{\ket{f}\}$.
\end{itemize}
Optically allowed transitions connect $\ket{g}\leftrightarrow\ket{n}$ and $\ket{n}\leftrightarrow\ket{f}$. In third-order spectroscopy the Liouville pathways do not contain populations of the $f$ manifold (they end in coherences such as $\rho_{fn}$), so relaxation out of $f$ typically enters effectively through additional dephasing/broadening of the $n\leftrightarrow f$ coherences.

\medskip

\textbf{Propagators in the secular approximation.}
In the secular approximation, the waiting-time evolution is expressed in terms of Liouville-space propagator elements of the generic form
\begin{equation}
	U_{nn,mm}(T),\qquad U_{nm,nm}(T),\qquad U_{gg,nn}(T),
	\label{eq:multilevel_propagators_thesis}
\end{equation}
describing population transfer within the excited manifold ($nn\to mm$), coherence dephasing within the excited manifold ($nm\to nm$), and refilling of the ground state from excited populations ($nn\to gg$). ESA pathways additionally involve propagators for $f$-manifold coherences (e.g. $U_{fn,fm}(T)$).

\medskip

\textbf{Recipe for constructing multilevel signals.}
Given these propagators, multilevel spectra are obtained by the same ``stitching'' procedure as in the two-level case:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item choose the relevant pulse sequence (and thus the allowed pathway classes and phase matching / phase cycling).
	\item for each pathway, write the product of four dipole matrix elements (e.g. $d_{ng}$, $d_{mg}$, and, for ESA, $d_{fn}$) and three propagators (coherence--population--coherence) appropriate to that pathway.
	\item Fourier transform over the detection time to obtain contributions as functions of $\omega_{\text{det}}$ (and, for 2D spectra, also over the coherence time to obtain $\omega_{\text{coh}}$).
	\item sum over all intermediate state indices $n,m,f,\dots$.
\end{enumerate}


This multilevel bookkeeping is exactly what becomes necessary once the system Hamiltonian contains multiple excited eigenstates (or excitonic bands) and the open-system Liouvillian generates non-trivial transfer and dephasing within these manifolds. The explicit multilevel formulation will therefore serve as the bridge from the pedagogical two-level picture to the full numerical simulations targeted in the later thesis chapters.

\medskip

\textbf{General dissipative channels (preview).}
For later use it is helpful to note that a multilevel relaxation model can be written compactly in Lindblad form by introducing one jump operator per allowed transition $\ket{n}\to\ket{m}$,
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{L}_{mn} = \sqrt{k_{mn}}\,\ket{m}\bra{n},
\end{equation*}
and, if needed, pure-dephasing operators for each level,
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{L}_{n}^{(\varphi)} = \sqrt{\gamma_{\varphi,n}}\,\ket{n}\bra{n}.
\end{equation*}
These ingredients define the Liouvillian whose propagator elements $U_{ab,cd}(t)$ enter directly in the pathway products described above.


In practice, one evaluates $S^{(3)}$ by summing the relevant pathways (often grouped into ground-state bleach, stimulated emission, and excited-state absorption). The experimentally separated rephasing and nonrephasing signals (\autoref{sec:three_pulse_geometry}) correspond to different subsets of these pathways, selected either by phase matching or by phase cycling.

\subsection{Wavevector phase-matching condition}
\label{subsec:phase_matching_condition}


In nonlinear optical processes, multiple light waves interact within a material to generate new frequency components. In a plane-wave picture, momentum conservation of the incident and generated fields implies that the signal wavevector $\boldsymbol{k}_S$ is given by a signed sum of the input wavevectors,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{k}_S = \pm\boldsymbol{k}_1 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_2 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_3.
	\label{eq:four_wave_mixing}
\end{equation}
This is the (wavevector) \emph{phase-matching condition}. The signs track whether the corresponding interaction involves the analytic (positive-frequency) part of the field or its complex conjugate (negative-frequency part) as introduced in~\autoref{eq:E_plusminus}. Equivalently, in Liouville space they reflect whether the field interaction acts on the ket (left action) or bra (right action) branch of the density matrix: absorption-like interactions contribute $+\boldsymbol{k}_i$, while emission-like/complex-conjugate interactions contribute $-\boldsymbol{k}_i$.

Different sign combinations correspond to different phase-matched signal directions and therefore to different subsets of nonlinear pathways. In noncollinear geometries these directions can be spatially separated, while in collinear geometries the same contributions can be isolated via their phase dependence (phase cycling, \autoref{sec:phase_cycling}). The two most important third-order phase-matching conditions for this thesis (rephasing and nonrephasing) are discussed below~\cite{freschetal2023twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.

\subsection{Representation of three ultrashort pulses}


The experimentally relevant case of three ultrashort pulses with carrier wavevectors $\boldsymbol{k}_1,\boldsymbol{k}_2,\boldsymbol{k}_3$ and carrier frequency $\omega_L$ is now considered. In the slowly varying envelope approximation,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	=
	\sum_{j=1}^{3}
	\Big[
	\boldsymbol{E}_j(t-t_j)\,e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_j\cdot\boldsymbol{r}-\omega_L t + \phi_j)}
	+ \text{c.c.}
	\Big],
	\label{eq:E_three_pulses_space}
\end{equation}
where $t_j$ is the arrival time of pulse $j$, $\phi_j$ is its carrier phase-kick, and $\boldsymbol{E}_j(t)$ is a slowly varying envelope (assumed identical spectral center $\omega_L$ for simplicity). The complex analytic signal is
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	=
	\sum_{j=1}^{3}
	\boldsymbol{E}_j(t-t_j)\,
	e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_j\cdot\boldsymbol{r}-\omega_L t + \phi_j)}.
\end{equation*}

The setup can be visualized in a noncollinear boxcar geometry as shown in~\autoref{fig:fwm_box_car_setup}. Here three pulses with wavevectors $\boldsymbol{k}_1$, $\boldsymbol{k}_2$, and $\boldsymbol{k}_3$ interact with the sample and generate a signal emitted into the phase-matched direction $\boldsymbol{k}_S$.

\subsection{Intuition: carrier phase-kicks and pulse area (delta-pulse picture)}
\label{subsec:phase_kick_pulse_area_intuition}

\textbf{Rotating-wave approximation (RWA).}
Near resonance, the electric field and dipole operator decompose as
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{E}(t)=\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}(t)+\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}(t), \qquad
    \boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}(t)=\big[\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}(t)\big]^*, \quad \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(+)}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(-)}.
    \label{eq:E_plusminus}
\end{equation}
Only the resonant cross terms $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(+)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(-)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ survive; the conjugate terms oscillate at $\propto e^{\pm 2i\omega t}$ and average away~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical,boyd2008chapter6nonlinear}. This assigns each interaction to either the positive-frequency component $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}\propto e^{-i\omega t}$ (absorption, ket-side) or negative-frequency component $\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}\propto e^{+i\omega t}$ (emission, bra-side), as indicated by Feynman-diagram arrow directions~\cite{hamm2005principlesnonlinearoptical}.

The phase parameters $\phi_j$ in~\autoref{eq:E_three_pulses_space} are often introduced as bookkeeping devices for phase cycling, but they also have a simple physical meaning: for a resonantly driven transition they set the \emph{optical phase} of the induced coherence and, equivalently, the \emph{rotation axis} of the pulse on the Bloch sphere.
This is most transparent for an effective two-level system $\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}$ within the rotating-wave approximation framework just outlined.
Writing the complex Rabi frequency of pulse $j$ as
\begin{equation}
	\Omega_j(t) \equiv \boldsymbol{\mu}_{eg}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}_j(t),
\end{equation}
the interaction Hamiltonian in the laser-rotating frame can be expressed (up to standard conventions) as
\begin{equation}
	\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int},j}(t)
	\;\propto\;
	\Omega_j(t-t_j)
	\Big( e^{-i\phi_j}\,\ket{e}\!\bra{g} + e^{+i\phi_j}\,\ket{g}\!\bra{e} \Big).
	\label{eq:Hint_phasekick}
\end{equation}
Thus, changing $\phi_j$ does not change the pulse \emph{intensity envelope} but rotates the complex drive between its in-phase and quadrature components. On the Bloch sphere this corresponds to a rotation around an axis in the equatorial ($x$--$y$) plane with azimuth angle $\phi_j$ (cf., e.g., the tutorial discussion in Ref.~\cite{grolletal2025fundamentalsheterodynewave}).

\medskip

The \emph{pulse area}
\begin{equation}
	\theta_j \;\equiv\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t\,\Omega_j(t-t_j)
	\label{eq:pulse_area_def}
\end{equation}
sets the corresponding rotation angle. In the idealized delta-pulse limit, one replaces $\Omega_j(t-t_j)\to \theta_j\,\delta(t-t_j)$, and the pulse acts as an instantaneous unitary
\begin{equation}
	\hat{U}_j
	\;=\;
	\exp\!\left[-\frac{i\,\theta_j}{2}\,\big(\cos\phi_j\,\hat{\tau}_x+\sin\phi_j\,\hat{\tau}_y\big)\right],
	\label{eq:delta_pulse_unitary}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\tau}_{x,y,z}$ denote the Pauli matrices, which rotates the Bloch vector by angle $\theta_j$ about the equatorial axis at azimuth $\phi_j$.
For example, a $\pi/2$ pulse ($\theta_j=\pi/2$) prepares a maximal coherence whose phase is set by $\phi_j$, while a $\pi$ pulse ($\theta_j=\pi$) inverts population.
In multi-pulse spectroscopy, the phases imprinted on successive coherences interfere. This is exactly why the macroscopic third-order polarization decomposes into phase-tagged components $\propto e^{i(l\phi_1+m\phi_2+n\phi_3)}$ in~\autoref{eq:P3_lmn}, enabling pathway selection by phase matching or phase cycling.

\subsection{Third-order polarization in space and time}


Inserting \autoref{eq:E_three_pulses_space} into the third-order convolution \autoref{eq:P3_convolution_thesis}, and keeping only terms resonant in RWA, one finds that the third-order polarization contains contributions oscillating with phase factors
\begin{equation*}
	\exp\!\Big[
		i(\pm\boldsymbol{k}_1 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_2 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_3)\cdot\boldsymbol{r}
		- i \omega_L t
		+ i(\pm \phi_1 \pm \phi_2 \pm \phi_3)
		\Big],
\end{equation*}
where each sign corresponds to an interaction with $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ (ket) or $\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ (bra). Thus the third-order polarization can be written as
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	=
	\sum_{l,m,n\in\{-1,0,+1\}}
	e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_S\cdot\boldsymbol{r} - \omega_L t + \Phi_{lmn})}\,
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t),
	\label{eq:P3_lmn}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{k}_S = l\,\boldsymbol{k}_1 + m\,\boldsymbol{k}_2 + n\,\boldsymbol{k}_3,\qquad
	\Phi_{lmn} = l\phi_1 + m\phi_2 + n\phi_3.
\end{equation*}
Only those combinations that satisfy energy and momentum (phase-matching) constraints contribute efficiently to measurable signals in a macroscopic sample~\cite{boyd2008chapter6nonlinear}.

\subsection{Rephasing and nonrephasing phase-matching conditions}
\label{subsec:rephasing_nonrephasing}


The phase-matched directions of interest for two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy are
\begin{align}
	\boldsymbol{k}_R    & = -\boldsymbol{k}_1 + \boldsymbol{k}_2 + \boldsymbol{k}_3, \label{eq:kR_def} \\
	\boldsymbol{k}_{NR} & = +\boldsymbol{k}_1 - \boldsymbol{k}_2 + \boldsymbol{k}_3,
	\label{eq:kNR_def}
\end{align}
usually identified as \emph{rephasing} (R) and \emph{nonrephasing} (NR) directions, respectively~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, freschetal2023twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}. In a noncollinear ``box'' geometry, beams are arranged such that different $\boldsymbol{k}_S$ point along distinct directions in space. A detector placed in the appropriate direction records the corresponding signal field.

\medskip

Besides the rotating-wave approximation, a further simplification is obtained in the \emph{impulsive limit} of well separated, ultrashort pulses: the interaction times are then strictly ordered and pulse overlap can be neglected~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, hamm2005principlesnonlinearoptical}. In this case the sign pattern in~\crefrange{eq:kR_def}{eq:kNR_def} can be read directly as the side (ket/bra) on which each interaction acts (cf.~the $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$/$\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ bookkeeping above and the phase-cycling geometry discussed in~\autoref{sec:phase_cycling}).


For the \textbf{rephasing} signal, $\boldsymbol{k}_R=-\boldsymbol{k}_1+\boldsymbol{k}_2+\boldsymbol{k}_3$, the first interaction is conjugated (bra-side, $-\boldsymbol{k}_1$), creating an optical coherence. The second pulse converts this coherence into a population. The third pulse generates a new coherence that radiates the signal. Because the phase evolution during the first coherence period is effectively reversed relative to the detection period, an inhomogeneously broadened ensemble can exhibit a photon-echo-like refocusing in the time domain around $t_{\mathrm{det}}\approx t_{\mathrm{coh}}$~\cite{jonas2003twodimensionalfemtosecondspectroscopy, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, freschetal2023twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.


By contrast, for the \textbf{nonrephasing} signal, $\boldsymbol{k}_{NR}=+\boldsymbol{k}_1-\boldsymbol{k}_2+\boldsymbol{k}_3$, the ordering corresponds to a ket-side first interaction ($+\boldsymbol{k}_1$), followed by a bra-side second interaction ($-\boldsymbol{k}_2$), and a ket-side third interaction ($+\boldsymbol{k}_3$). The coherence evolution does not reverse in the same way, so static disorder is not refocused and no echo is formed.

The rephasing signal is sensitive to inhomogeneous broadening and exhibits echo-like refocusing, whereas the nonrephasing signal does not rephase static disorder and emphasizes homogeneous dynamics. Their sum, properly phased, yields an absorptive 2D spectrum with well-defined peak shapes.

Other third-order directions can be accessed by alternative phase-matching or phase-cycling selections (e.g.\ double-quantum coherence signals, often written as $\boldsymbol{k}_S=+\boldsymbol{k}_1+\boldsymbol{k}_2-\boldsymbol{k}_3$ in the usual convention), but they are not required for the aims of this thesis. See Ref.~\cite{caietal2024extractingdoublequantumcoherence} for a broader overview.

\section{Phase Cycling and Selection of Pathways}
\label{sec:phase_cycling}


Instead of spatially resolving different $\boldsymbol{k}_S$-components, one can use a collinear arrangement and separate contributions by varying the carrier phases $\phi_j$ and performing a discrete Fourier analysis with respect to $(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)$~\cite{hamm2005principlesnonlinearoptical, greenetal2024vibrationalcoherenceshalfbroadband}. This is particularly useful for numerical simulations and for experiments where spatial selection is not practical.


Operationally, phase cycling replaces spatial separation by a set of repeated measurements (or simulation runs) in which the excitation phases are stepped through a discrete cycle. Because different Liouville-space pathways carry different phase factors, suitable linear combinations --- equivalently, a discrete Fourier transform with respect to the phases --- isolate the desired contribution. A common choice is to step phases in increments of $\pi/2$ (or, more generally, $2\pi/N_\phi$), which is sufficient to separate the third-order components relevant for rephasing and nonrephasing 2D spectra.

The collinear phase-cycling geometry is shown in~\autoref{fig:fwm_phase_cycling_setup}.

	\caption{Schematic four-wave-mixing setup in a collinear, phase-cycling geometry. Spatial separation of different $\boldsymbol{k}_S$ components is replaced by varying the carrier phases of the input pulses and extracting the desired contribution via phase cycling.}
	\label{fig:fwm_phase_cycling_setup}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Phase-Cycling Fourier Selection and Construction of 2D Spectra}
\label{subsec:phase_cycling_fourier_selection}


In general, the nonlinear polarization generated by three input fields can be decomposed into wavevector-tagged components,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{P}^{\mathrm{NL}}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	=
	\sum_{l,m,n}
	e^{i\,(l\boldsymbol{k}_1+m\boldsymbol{k}_2+n\boldsymbol{k}_3)\cdot \boldsymbol{r}}\,\boldsymbol{P}_{l,m,n}(t)
	+\mathrm{c.c.},
	\label{eq:total_polarization_sum}
\end{equation}
where the physically relevant third-order directions include the rephasing and nonrephasing combinations in~\cref{eq:kR_def,eq:kNR_def}. In a collinear geometry these components cannot be spatially separated, but they can be isolated through their dependence on the carrier phases $\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3$ (phase cycling).


At fixed observation direction (e.g.\ collinear), the total complex third-order polarization can be written as a Fourier series in the phases,
\begin{equation*}
	P^{(3)}(t;\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)
	=
	\sum_{l,m,n} e^{i(l\phi_1+m\phi_2+n\phi_3)}\,P^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t).
\end{equation*}
Here $P^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t)$ collects the third-order contributions with a fixed combination of pulse phases. In particular, the rephasing and nonrephasing components correspond to
\begin{align}
	\text{rephasing:}\quad (l,m,n)    & = (-1,+1,+1),
	\label{eq:phase_rephasing}                        \\
	\text{nonrephasing:}\quad (l,m,n) & = (+1,-1,+1).
	\label{eq:phase_nonrephasing}
\end{align}
These index triples are the phase analogues of the wavevector combinations~\cref{eq:kR_def,eq:kNR_def}~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, greenetal2024vibrationalcoherenceshalfbroadband}.

\subsection{Discrete phase cycling}


In practice, a finite set of phase values is used, typically $\phi_j\in \{0,2\pi/N_\phi,\dots,2\pi (N_\phi-1)/N_\phi\}$ for each $j$. The desired component is then obtained by discrete Fourier projection
\begin{equation}
	P^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t)
	\simeq
	\frac{1}{N_\phi^3}
	\sum_{\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3}
	e^{-i(l\phi_1+m\phi_2+n\phi_3)}\,
	P^{(3)}(t;\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3).
	\label{eq:discrete_phase_cycling}
\end{equation}
In the continuous limit, the corresponding inverse Fourier transform reads
\begin{align}
	P^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t)
	 & =
	\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}
	\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}
	d\phi_1\,d\phi_2\,d\phi_3\;
	e^{-i(l\phi_1+m\phi_2+n\phi_3)}\,
	P^{(3)}(t;\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3).
	\label{eq:continuous_phase_cycling}
\end{align}

In simulations (and in this thesis), $P^{(3)}(t)$ is obtained by solving the full master equation including the time-dependent light--matter interaction at each phase setting $(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)$ and then extracting the phase-selected third-order component via~\autoref{eq:discrete_phase_cycling} with $(l,m,n)$ chosen according to~\cref{eq:phase_rephasing,eq:phase_nonrephasing}.

\medskip

\textbf{Remark (order selection in simulations).}
Besides phase cycling, another common numerical strategy is to isolate a desired nonlinear order by combining runs with different subsets of pulses turned on (and subtracting lower-order contributions). In this thesis phase cycling is used, because it selects the desired rephasing/nonrephasing pathways directly through their phase dependence.

The corresponding complex signal fields are proportional to these selected polarizations,
\begin{equation}
	E_{S,R/NR}(t) \propto i\,P^{(3)}_{R/NR}(t),
	\label{eq:field_polarization_relation}
\end{equation}
where proportionality constants depend on sample geometry and propagation but do not affect the normalized 2D spectral shapes considered here. This proportionality holds within the slowly varying envelope approximation~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, jonas2003twodimensionalfemtosecondspectroscopy}.

\section{Construction of Two-Dimensional Spectra}
\label{sec:2D_construction_formal}


In this thesis the phase-cycled complex signal field $E_{S,R/NR}(t_{\text{coh}},T,t_{\text{det}})$ is treated as the object from which 2D spectra are constructed. Experimentally, this quantity is accessed by heterodyne detection: the detected intensity contains an interference term that is linear in the signal field,
\begin{equation*}
	I_{\mathrm{det}}(\omega_{\text{det}})
	\propto
	\big|E_{\mathrm{LO}}(\omega_{\text{det}}) + E_S(\omega_{\text{det}})\big|^2
	\approx
	|E_{\mathrm{LO}}(\omega_{\text{det}})|^2
	+ 2\,\Re\!\left[E_{\mathrm{LO}}^*(\omega_{\text{det}})E_S(\omega_{\text{det}})\right]
\end{equation*}
for $|E_S|\ll|E_{\mathrm{LO}}|$ after subtraction of the LO background. The detailed discussion of homodyne/heterodyne detection, phase sensitivity, and phasing is deferred to~\autoref{app:detection_schemes}. The Maxwell-side propagation and phase-matching assumptions underpinning $E_S\propto P^{(3)}$ are derived in~\autoref{app:2dfts_maxwell}.

\subsection{Time variables for 2D spectroscopy}


In a three-pulse experiment, it is customary to express the signal in terms of the three time delays
\begin{equation*}
	t_{\text{coh}}\equiv\tau_1,\qquad
	T\equiv\tau_2,\qquad
	t_{\text{det}}\equiv\tau_3,
\end{equation*}
with delay variables defined in~\autoref{eq:app_change_of_variables} and used in the third-order convolution~\autoref{eq:app_Pn_convolution}. The quantities $t_{\text{coh}}$, $T$, and $t_{\text{det}}$ are referred to as the coherence time, waiting time, and detection time, respectively.
Scanning the waiting time $T$ probes population dynamics and spectral diffusion during the population interval between the second and third interactions~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}.

\textbf{Remark (positive vs.\ negative coherence time).}
In some presentations one also considers negative $t_{\text{coh}}$ values, which correspond to reversing the order of pulses 1 and 2. In that case the rephasing photon-echo picture does not apply. Instead one obtains a nonrephasing free-induction--decay-like contribution that is complementary to the $t_{\text{coh}}>0$ rephasing branch~\cite{ginsbergetal2009twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.
For each fixed waiting time $T$, the signal is a function of the two coherence times $t_{\text{coh}}$ and $t_{\text{det}}$
\begin{equation*}
	E_{S,R/NR} = E_{S,R/NR}(t_{\text{coh}},T,t_{\text{det}}).
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Double Fourier transform and 2D spectra}


After computing the time-domain polarization (\autoref{eq:P_def_spectroscopy}) and selecting the desired phase-tagged component via phase cycling (\autoref{eq:continuous_phase_cycling}), one obtains the corresponding complex signal field $E_{S,R/NR}(t_{\text{coh}},T,t_{\text{det}})$ (\autoref{eq:field_polarization_relation}). For the non-perturbative numerical extraction, see~\autoref{eq:third_order_signal_numerical} in~\autoref{app:numerical_implementation}. The two-dimensional spectrum is then defined as the double Fourier transform of the time-domain signal with respect to $(t_{\text{coh}},t_{\text{det}})$ at fixed $T$. The opposite sign in the $t_{\text{coh}}$-exponent distinguishes rephasing (photon-echo) and nonrephasing branches~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, greenetal2024vibrationalcoherenceshalfbroadband}.

In the numerical implementation, slowly varying complex envelopes are used (the optical carrier $e^{-i\omega_L t}$ is removed), so $\omega_{\text{coh}}$ and $\omega_{\text{det}}$ represent Fourier variables of these envelopes.

For the rephasing signal,
\begin{equation*}
	S_R(\omega_{\text{coh}},T,\omega_{\text{det}})
	=
	\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\text{det}}
	\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\text{coh}}\;\Theta(t_{\text{det}})\,\Theta(t_{\text{coh}})\,
	e^{+i\omega_{\text{coh}} t_{\text{coh}}}\,e^{-i\omega_{\text{det}} t_{\text{det}}}\,
	E_{S,R}(t_{\text{coh}},T,t_{\text{det}}),
\end{equation*}
while for the nonrephasing signal,
\begin{equation*}
	S_{NR}(\omega_{\text{coh}},T,\omega_{\text{det}})
	=
	\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\text{det}}
	\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\text{coh}}\;\Theta(t_{\text{det}})\,\Theta(t_{\text{coh}})\,
	e^{-i\omega_{\text{coh}} t_{\text{coh}}}\,e^{-i\omega_{\text{det}} t_{\text{det}}}\,
	E_{S,NR}(t_{\text{coh}},T,t_{\text{det}}).
\end{equation*}
In numerical implementations, finite integration limits (discrete time grids) are used and discrete Fourier transforms are applied with appropriate windowing. On uniform grids these are evaluated efficiently via (shifted) fast Fourier transforms~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, greenetal2024vibrationalcoherenceshalfbroadband}.

The \emph{absorptive} 2D spectrum is then obtained as the real part of the sum
\begin{equation*}
	S_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega_{\text{coh}},T,\omega_{\text{det}})
	=
	\Re\Big\{ S_R(\omega_{\text{coh}},T,\omega_{\text{det}}) + S_{NR}(\omega_{\text{coh}},T,\omega_{\text{det}}) \Big\}.
\end{equation*}
This combination removes dispersive distortions that appear when considering $|S_R|$ or $|S_{NR}|$ alone and yields peak shapes directly comparable with experimental absorptive 2D spectra~\cite{jonas2003twodimensionalfemtosecondspectroscopy, fullerogilvie2015experimentalimplementationstwodimensional}.
