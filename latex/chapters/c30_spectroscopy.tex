\section{Motivation and Historical Development}
\label{sec:spctr_spectroscopy_motivation}
Spectroscopy studies how matter interacts with energy, most commonly through absorption, emission, or scattering of radiation~\cite{skoogetal2017principlesinstrumentalanalysis}.
Because atoms and molecules occupy discrete energy levels, optical transitions satisfy the Planck relation $\Delta E=h\nu=hc/\lambda$.
Early spectroscopy exploited this to identify characteristic line spectra that served as fingerprints of quantized structure~\cite{balmer1885notizuberspectrallinien, rydberg1890xxxivstructurelinespectra, bohr1913constitutionatomsmolecules}.
Quantum mechanics later connected these lines to transitions between eigenstates of the microscopic Hamiltonian~\cite{berman2011principleslaserspectroscopy, dirac1927quantumtheoryemission}.
In practice, transitions are often labelled by the wavenumber $\tilde{\nu}=1/\lambda\,[\mathrm{cm}^{-1}]$, which is proportional to the transition energy.
The electronic transitions studied in this thesis lie in the visible and near-UV range, $\tilde{\nu}\sim 13\,000$--$25\,000\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. In this regime, nonlinear optical spectroscopy probes molecular dynamics on their intrinsic electronic timescales, typically a few tens of femtoseconds ($\tau_{\mathrm{typical}}\sim 10$--$50\,\mathrm{fs}$), enabling an effective real-time view of ultrafast motion.

For decades, spectroscopy was essentially linear: a weak field probes the sample and one records intensity as a function of frequency.
Linear absorption and emission reveal excitation energies, oscillator strengths, and broadening mechanisms, such as natural linewidth or Doppler broadening~\cite{skoogetal2017principlesinstrumentalanalysis}.
However, a one-dimensional line shape compresses distinct dynamical processes---coherence loss, population transfer, and environment-induced frequency fluctuations---into a single spectral profile.
This makes different microscopic mechanisms difficult to disentangle.

The induced polarisation $\boldsymbol{P}(t)$ contains not only components linear in the applied field $\boldsymbol{E}(t)$~\cite{kubo1957statisticalmechanicaltheoryirreversible},
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}(t) = \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,
\boldsymbol{R}^{(1)}(\tau)\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}(t-\tau),
\end{equation*}
where $\boldsymbol{R}^{(1)}$ is the causal linear-response kernel.
There are also quadratic, cubic, and higher-order contributions,
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}(t) \propto \boldsymbol{E}(t)^n,\qquad n\ge 2.
\end{equation*}

These terms generate effects such as second-harmonic generation, four-wave mixing, and stimulated Raman scattering.
Conceptually, they probe multi-time correlation functions of the system.
Therefore, they encode dynamical information that is inaccessible in the linear regime~\cite{frankenetal1961generationopticalharmonics, mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}.

Two-dimensional Fourier-transform spectroscopy is a powerful tool within nonlinear spectroscopy.
By correlating excitation and detection frequencies, it separates homogeneous from inhomogeneous broadening.
Homogeneous broadening reflects the intrinsic linewidth of a single chromophore, set by coherence loss and population relaxation.
Inhomogeneous broadening, by contrast, arises from static disorder in the molecular environment (e.g.\ different binding sites or solvent configurations), which distributes transition frequencies across the ensemble.
This separation allows the homogeneous linewidth to be extracted even in strongly inhomogeneously broadened systems~\cite{bangertetal2022highresolutiontwodimensionalelectronic, freschetal2023twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.
In addition, two-dimensional spectra track spectral diffusion---time-dependent fluctuations of transition frequencies caused by a slowly evolving local environment---through the loss of frequency--frequency correlation with waiting time~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy,mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}.
Cross peaks further visualize couplings between transitions and energy-transfer pathways, which are central to the analyses in this thesis.

This thesis uses the response-function formalism to connect these experimental capabilities to the theoretical framework developed in~\autoref{chapter:oqs_open_quantum_systems}.
The approach presented here follows~\cite{mancal2022nonlinearopticalspectroscopy}.
The response-function formalism links a given pulse sequence to the measured signal via the induced polarisation.
By separating matter and field contributions, this formalism makes the perturbative order explicit.
The matter-side derivation---how incident fields generate $\boldsymbol{P}^{(n)}$---is given in~\autoref{app:response_functions} and shows how open-system master equations, such as the Redfield equation (microscopically derived in~\autoref{sec:oqs_Redfield_eq}), enter spectroscopy.
The field-side derivation---how $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ radiates the signal---is given in~\autoref{app:2dfts_maxwell}.

The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows.
\autoref{sec:spctr_light_matter} introduces the semiclassical light--matter Hamiltonian in the dipole approximation, which establishes the fundamental microscopic interaction governing how laser pulses perturb molecular systems and provides the starting point for all spectroscopic calculations in this thesis.
\autoref{sec:spctr_pol_and_macr_response} provides a unified summary of the two technical appendices by combining the matter-side response expansion with the Maxwell-side radiation step into one signal chain.
\autoref{sec:spctr_response_formalism} then states the core response-function expressions and pathway structure used in the spectroscopy workflow.
Finally, \autoref{sec:spctr_three_pulse_geometry}--\autoref{sec:spctr_2D_construction_formal} applies these ingredients to three-pulse experiments, phase matching, phase cycling, and 2D spectrum construction.

\section{Light--Matter Interaction}
\label{sec:spctr_light_matter}
Throughout this thesis, the electromagnetic field is treated as a classical, time-dependent drive, while the matter degrees of freedom are described quantum mechanically.
The central object is the density matrix of the matter, whose evolution determines the optical response.

The microscopic dynamics of the full system (molecule plus environment) are governed by the Liouville--von Neumann equation (cf.~\autoref{eq:oqs_Von_Neumann_Equation})
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{T}}(t) = -i\,[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{T}}(t)],
\end{equation*}
with total Hamiltonian
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{E}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}.
\end{equation*}
Here $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{E}}$ is the environment Hamiltonian and $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}$ the system--environment coupling.
The semiclassical light field enters only through the time-dependent system Hamiltonian $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)$.
The structure therefore is a time-dependent version of the one used in~\autoref{sec:oqs_setup_system_environment}.

\subsection{Minimal Coupling}
\label{subsec:spctr_minimal_coupling}

In Coulomb gauge, the interaction of a particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ with a classical electromagnetic field is described by the minimal-coupling Hamiltonian
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}, t)
	= \frac{1}{2m}\Bigl[\hat{\boldsymbol{p}} + q\,\boldsymbol{A}(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},t)\Bigr]^2
	+ V(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}) - q\,\phi(\hat{\boldsymbol{r}},t),
\end{equation*}
where $\boldsymbol{A}$ and $\phi$ are the vector and scalar potentials of the external field, $V$ is the static binding potential, $\boldsymbol{p}$ is the canonical momentum operator, and $\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}$ is the position operator.
The transverse electric field is related to the vector potential by
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{E}_\perp(\boldsymbol{r},t)=-\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r},t)}{\partial t}.
\end{equation*}

In most optical settings the wavelength is much larger than the molecular size, so the field varies negligibly across the system.
In this limit, a unitary transformation leads to an equivalent effective Hamiltonian
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)=\hat{H}_0 - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}(t),
\end{equation*}
with $\hat{H}_0$ the field-free Hamiltonian and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=-q\,\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}$ the electric dipole operator.
For a fixed linear polarisation direction $\boldsymbol{e}$, define
\begin{equation*}
	E(t)=\boldsymbol{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}(t),
	\qquad
	\hat{\mu}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\boldsymbol{e},
\end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation}
	\hat{H}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)=\hat{H}_0 - \hat{\mu}\,E(t).
\label{eq:spctr_semiclassical_hamiltonian_scalar}
\end{equation}
Details of the derivation can be found in many textbooks  such as~\cite{cohen-tannoudjietal2008atomphotoninteractionsbasic,berman2011principleslaserspectroscopy}.

A key simplification used throughout is the rotating-wave approximation (RWA).
It is introduced here and defined again in~\autoref{eq:spctr_E_plusminus}. Its role in pathway selection is discussed in~\autoref{subsec:spctr_rephasing_nonrephasing}.
For narrow-linewidth transitions driven near resonance, the field is decomposed into positive- and negative-frequency components $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ and $\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$, and only the near-resonant cross terms $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(+)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(-)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ remain in the equation of motion.

\section{Microscopic and Macroscopic Response}
\label{sec:spctr_pol_and_macr_response}
This section summarizes, in one place, the two complementary derivations developed in the appendices. The next sections then build on this summary to detail the response-function formalism and its application to 2D spectroscopy.

\subsection{Single-Molecule Dipole Expectation Value}
For a single molecule or chromophore, the microscopic induced dipole is given by the expectation value of the electric dipole operator,
\begin{equation}
\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rangle(t)
= \mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\!\bigl[
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)
\bigr].
\label{eq:spctr_P_def_spectroscopy}
\end{equation}
Here $\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{E}}\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{T}}(t)$ is the reduced density matrix obtained by tracing out the environment from the total state, and it follows the reduced open-system dynamics introduced in~\autoref{chapter:oqs_open_quantum_systems}.
In practice, dissipative effects like population relaxation and dephasing enter through this reduced evolution, which provides the dynamical input to the matter-side response derivation in~\autoref{app:response_functions}.
Physically, $\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rangle(t)$ quantifies the laser-driven redistribution of charge within the molecule.
The field induces charge displacements, and these microscopic motions are the origin of the polarisation that ultimately acts as a source for the emitted electromagnetic signal.

\subsection{Macroscopic Polarisation Density}
\label{spctr:sec:macroscopic_polarisation}
The macroscopic polarisation field $\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t)$ that appears in Maxwell's equations is obtained from the microscopic molecular dipole moments through ensemble averaging.
For a dilute sample with number density $N$ (molecules per unit volume), the macroscopic polarisation density at position $\boldsymbol{r}$ is given by
\begin{equation*}
    \boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
    \approx N\,\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rangle(t),
\end{equation*}
where the right-hand side represents an ensemble average over an appropriate statistical distribution of molecular states.
In condensed-phase samples, the ensemble may include averages over orientational distributions, local environments, or disorder realizations.
This coarse-graining procedure effectively replaces the discrete molecular dipoles by a continuous polarisation field: on a microscopic level, charges $q_i$ at positions $\boldsymbol{r}_i(t)$ contribute a polarisation density
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)
		\sim \sum_i q_i \boldsymbol{r}_i(t)\, \delta(\boldsymbol{r} - \boldsymbol{r}_i(t)),
\end{equation*}
and spatial averaging over a volume that is large compared to molecular dimensions but small on macroscopic scales yields the smooth macroscopic field~\cite{mancal2022nonlinearopticalspectroscopy}.
Accordingly, $\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rangle(t)$ denotes the single-molecule dipole expectation value, whereas $\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t)$ denotes the ensemble-averaged polarisation density sourced in Maxwell's equations. For simplicity, when only pathway structure is at issue, the remainder of this chapter suppresses the density and ensemble-averaging prefactors and uses $\boldsymbol{P}$ as shorthand for the same response up to that macroscopic conversion.

In the perturbative regime, the macroscopic polarisation is expanded as
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
=\boldsymbol{P}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
+\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)+\cdots.
\end{equation*}
In many applications, when the ensemble prefactor $N$ is not central to the discussion of dynamics or pathway selection, it is suppressed in the notation.
Thus, formulas involving $\boldsymbol{P}$ and equations involving $\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rangle$ may appear side-by-side without explicit ensemble-averaging factors.
In the Maxwell-side summary below, the same convention as in~\autoref{app:2dfts_maxwell} is used: natural units with $\hbar=c=1$ and Heaviside--Lorentz electrodynamics, so vacuum constants are absorbed into the field and source definitions.
The field-side derivation then yields the wave equation with polarisation source term,
\begin{equation*}
	\nabla \times \nabla \times \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)
	+ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}
	\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)
	= - \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}
	\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{r}, t).
\end{equation*}
In a four-wave-mixing experiment with heterodyne detection (see~\autoref{sec:spctr_detection_schemes}), the recorded quantity is linear in the emitted field and therefore in the selected third-order polarisation component,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	\propto
	i\,\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t).
	\label{eq:spctr_signal_field_from_polarisation}
\end{equation}
This closes the link between microscopic dynamics and the measured signal: the material model determines $\boldsymbol{R}^{(3)}$, $\boldsymbol{R}^{(3)}$ determines $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$, and Maxwell propagation/detection map $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$ to the observable field.
The combined result of both appendices is the practical spectroscopy chain used throughout this thesis: the third-order response function $\boldsymbol{R}^{(3)}$ determines the third-order polarisation $\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}$, which generates the emitted signal field $\boldsymbol{E}_{\mathrm{S}}$, from which an interpretable spectrum $\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\mathrm{S}}$ is obtained.
\autoref{sec:spctr_response_formalism} now details the first link, i.e.
the response-function construction of $\boldsymbol{R}^{(3)}$ and its pathway decomposition.

\section{Response Function Formalism}
\label{sec:spctr_response_formalism}

In the collinear geometry (which is used in this thesis, see~\autoref{sec:spctr_phase_cycling}), both the excitation and detection are linearly polarised along a fixed laboratory direction. This choice of geometry allows us to work directly with scalar field amplitudes $E(t)$ and a single dipole component $\hat{\mu}$ throughout this section, rather than the full vector quantities that would be needed for arbitrary polarisation states.

Experiments probe isotropic ensembles, so strict comparison with measured amplitudes generally requires orientational averages of dipole--polarisation products (second-rank for linear response, fourth-rank for third-order signals). Because all simulations below use fixed linear polarisations and the later analysis focuses on spectral structure and dynamics rather than absolute signal amplitudes, orientational factors enter only as external prefactors and are therefore not developed.
Also, topics such as pump--probe spectroscopy, magic-angle conditions, and the full time--frequency trade-off of finite pulses are compressed here to the elements needed for the 2D pathway construction used in the remainder of the thesis. These can be found in more comprehensive treatments such as~\cite{mancal2022nonlinearopticalspectroscopy}.

The response-function formalism connects the applied field $E(t)$ to the induced polarisation $P^{(n)}(t)$.
%The full derivation is given in~\autoref{app:response_functions}. 
Here the key results are stated for linear response ($n=1$) and, most importantly for this thesis, for third-order response ($n=3$).

Linear response is the simplest setting in which the causal time-domain structure is explicit and the Liouville-space pathway structure first appears.
For these reasons, it is discussed in detail.

\subsection{Linear Response and the Kubo Formula}
\label{sec:spctr_linear_response}
Specialising~\autoref{eq:app_Pn_convolution_scalar} and~\autoref{eq:app_Sn_def_scalar} to $n=1$ gives
\begin{equation}
P^{(1)}(t)
= \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1\,R^{(1)}(\tau_1)\,E(t-\tau_1),
\label{eq:spctr_P1_convolution_thesis}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
R^{(1)}(\tau_1)
= \mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl\{\hat{\mu}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\bigr\},
\label{eq:spctr_S1_def_superop_thesis}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{U}_0(t)$ denotes the field-free Liouville-space propagator and $\mathcal{V}[A] = [\hat{\mu},A]$ is the commutator of $A$ with the dipole operator.

Even at first order, \autoref{eq:spctr_S1_def_superop_thesis} contains two Liouville-space pathways.
Expanding the commutator gives a difference of two time-ordered dipole correlation functions, which motivates defining
\begin{equation}
	J_{1}(\tau) \equiv \mathrm{Tr}\bigl\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\bigr\},
	\label{eq:spctr_J1_definitions}
\end{equation}
so that the causal response can be written as
\begin{equation}
	R^{(1)}(\tau) = i\,\Theta(\tau)\,\bigl[J_{1}(\tau)-J_{1}^*(\tau)\bigr].
	\label{eq:spctr_R1_J1_J1*}
\end{equation}
The two terms correspond to interactions on the ket and on the bra, with a relative minus sign.

To make this structure explicit, consider the simplest example of a two-level system.
This allows the general expressions to be evaluated in closed form and introduces the double-sided Feynman representation that will later be used for higher-order processes (see~\autoref{sec:spctr_third_order_response}).

\subsubsection{Two-Level System}
\label{sec:spctr_two_level_system_linear_response}
Consider a system with two electronic states, $\ket{g}$ and $\ket{e}$.
As the simplest nontrivial benchmark, this two-level limit already contains the basic spectroscopy workflow from pulse sequence to induced polarisation and rephasing/nonrephasing response.
Although it cannot capture excited-state absorption into a higher manifold, it is the natural reference model for later comparisons with multilevel systems.
Choosing the ground-state energy as zero, the field-free Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
	\hat{H}_{0}^{(\mathrm{TLS})} = \omega_{eg}\,\ket{e}\!\bra{e},
	\label{eq:spctr_tls_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
with transition frequency $\omega_{eg}$.
The optical dipole operator projected onto the chosen polarisation direction is written as
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\mu}^{(\mathrm{TLS})}
	= \mu_{ge}\,\ket{g}\!\bra{e} + \mu_{eg}\,\ket{e}\!\bra{g},
	\qquad \mu_{eg}=\mu_{ge}^*.
	\label{eq:spctr_tls_dipole}
\end{equation}
For optical transitions with $\omega_{eg}$ corresponding to $\tilde{\nu}_{eg} \approx 16\,000\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, the excited-state thermal population at room temperature is negligible.
Therefore, the initial condition can be approximated as the electronic ground state,
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}} = \ket{g}\bra{g}.
\end{equation*}
For pathway bookkeeping it is convenient to factor out the transition dipole amplitude and write
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{\mu} \equiv \hat{\mu}^{(\mathrm{TLS})}=d\,\hat{m} \quad\text{with}\quad
	\hat{m} = \ket{e}\bra{g} + \ket{g}\bra{e}.
\end{equation*}
In the pedagogical pathway equations below, only the state structure is emphasised, so overall dipole prefactors are suppressed.
The pathway logic is already visible here: a commutator produces a left and a right action with a relative minus sign.
Expanding the first commutator converts the equilibrium state $\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}$ into a superposition of optical coherences,
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{V}[\ket{g}\!\bra{g}]
	\propto \ket{e}\!\bra{g} - \ket{g}\!\bra{e}.
	\label{eq:spctr_S1_commutator_expanded}
\end{equation}
During the first delay $\tau$, the coherences undergo free evolution.
In the secular approximation, they evolve independently with the Green function
\begin{equation}
	G_{eg}(\tau) = \exp\bigl[-(\mathrm{i}\omega_{eg}+\Gamma_{eg})\tau\bigr],
	\label{eq:spctr_Green_function_two_level}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_{eg}=E_e-E_g$ and $\Gamma_{eg}$ is the phenomenological optical dephasing rate. Applying the free-evolution propagator to each coherence then gives
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{U}_0(\tau)[\ket{e}\!\bra{g}]\approx G_{eg}(\tau)\,\ket{e}\!\bra{g},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{U}_0(\tau)[\ket{g}\!\bra{e}]\approx G_{eg}^*(\tau)\,\ket{g}\!\bra{e}.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Double-Sided Feynman Diagrams}
\label{sec:spctr_dsfd}
The left/right action distinction encoded in $\mathcal{V}[\hat{A}] \equiv [\hat{\mu},\hat{A}]$ generates distinct pathways with characteristic sign patterns.
These pathways are visualised by double-sided Feynman diagrams in~\autoref{fig:spctr_linear_response_diagrams}.
The construction rules are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Two vertical lines represent the ket (left) and bra (right) branches of $\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}$. Time flows upward.
	\item Both branches start in thermal equilibrium $\ket{g}$.
	\item Straight arrows mark interactions, i.e.\ actions of $\mathcal{V}$.
	\item Curly lines represent readout by $\hat{m}$, returning the system to $\ket{g}\bra{g}$.
	Only closed diagrams contribute to the signal.
	\item Each right interaction contributes a factor $-1$, which fixes the overall sign.
	\item Coherences carry optical phase factors ($G_{eg}(\tau)$ for $\ket{e}\bra{g}$, $G_{eg}^*(\tau)$ for $\ket{g}\bra{e}$). Populations are approximately stationary.
	\item Under the RWA, each interaction is associated with $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ or $\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$, which determines the phase-matching direction (see~\autoref{subsec:spctr_phase_matching_condition}).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Frequency-Domain Susceptibility and Absorption}
The linear susceptibility is the Fourier transform of the causal response kernel \autoref{eq:spctr_R1_J1_J1*},
\begin{equation*}
	\chi^{(1)}(\omega)
	= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,R^{(1)}(\tau)\,
	\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}
	= \int_0^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,R^{(1)}(\tau)\,
	\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}.
\end{equation*}
With this, \autoref{eq:spctr_P1_convolution_thesis} becomes in frequency space
\begin{equation*}
		P^{(1)}(\omega) = \chi^{(1)}(\omega)E(\omega).
\end{equation*}
Conventional line shapes can be connected to the pathway functions $J_1(\tau)$ and $J_1^*(\tau)$ defined in~\autoref{eq:spctr_J1_definitions}, by approximating the coherence pathway through the Green function, $J_1(\tau)\propto G_{eg}(\tau)$.
This yields
\begin{equation*}
	\int_0^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,
	G_{eg}(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}
	= \frac{1}{\Gamma_{eg}+\mathrm{i}(\omega_{eg}-\omega)},
\end{equation*}
and therefore the familiar complex Lorentzian susceptibility
\begin{equation*}
	\chi^{(1)}(\omega)
	\propto d^2
	\frac{1}{\Gamma_{eg} - \mathrm{i}(\omega-\omega_{eg})}.
\end{equation*}
Its imaginary part gives the absorptive line shape centred at $\omega_{eg}$ with half-width $\Gamma_{eg}$, and the absorption coefficient satisfies $\alpha(\omega)\propto \operatorname{Im}\chi^{(1)}(\omega)$.
For multiple excited states $e_i$, the total absorption is a sum over allowed $g\to e_i$ transitions.

\subsection{Nonlinear Response}
\label{sec:spctr_third_order_response}
Even-order susceptibilities vanish in centrosymmetric media, so the lowest-order nonlinear response relevant here is third order~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical,mancal2022nonlinearopticalspectroscopy}.
The central $\chi^{(3)}$ process for this thesis is four-wave mixing (FWM), in which three incident fields generate a signal field.
The resulting photon-echo and free-induction-decay contributions form the basis of 2D spectroscopy.

\subsubsection{Third-Order Response Function \texorpdfstring{$R^{(3)}$}{R(3)}}
Inserting $n=3$ into~\autoref{eq:app_Pn_convolution_scalar} and~\autoref{eq:app_Sn_def_scalar} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spctr_P3_convolution_thesis}
P^{(3)}(t)
= \int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_3\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_2\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\tau_1\,
R^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)\,
E(t-\tau_3)\,E(t-\tau_3-\tau_2)\,E(t-\tau_3-\tau_2-\tau_1),
\end{equation}
with response function
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spctr_S3_def_thesis}
R^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
= (\mathrm{i})^3\,\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl\{\hat{\mu}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_3)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_2)\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_0(\tau_1)\,\mathcal{V}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]\Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
This third-order response contains the multi-time correlations central to the present thesis.
Reading the operator product in~\autoref{eq:spctr_S3_def_thesis} from right to left corresponds to the causal sequence: start from $\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}$, apply a dipole interaction, evolve freely, apply another interaction, and so on, followed by the dipole readout at time $t$.

\subsubsection{Two-Level System and Pathway Expansion}
\label{sec:spctr_two_level_third_order_pathways}
To see concretely how higher-order expressions expand, consider again the two-level system from \autoref{sec:spctr_two_level_system_linear_response}.
After the first interaction $\mathcal{V}$, $\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}$ is a linear combination of the coherences $\ket{e}\!\bra{g}$ and $\ket{g}\!\bra{e}$, followed by free evolution during $\tau_1$, which imprints phase factors $G_{eg}(\tau_1)$ and $G_{eg}^*(\tau_1)$ (see~\autoref{eq:spctr_Green_function_two_level,eq:spctr_S1_commutator_expanded}).
A second light--matter interaction converts these coherences into populations,
\begin{equation*}
	\mathcal{V}[\ket{e}\!\bra{g}] \propto \ket{g}\!\bra{g} - \ket{e}\!\bra{e},
    \qquad
	\mathcal{V}[\ket{g}\!\bra{e}] \propto \ket{e}\!\bra{e} - \ket{g}\!\bra{g}.
\end{equation*}
During $\tau_2$, the density operator is (in this simple model) a linear combination of populations.
The third commutator converts populations back into coherences, which evolve during $\tau_3$.
Finally, left multiplication by $\hat{m}$ and the trace yield the measurable polarisation.

Writing the nested commutator~\autoref{eq:spctr_S3_def_thesis} explicitly,
\begin{equation*}
	R^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
	=(-i)\,
	\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,[\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}),[\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1),[\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}]]]\Big\},
\end{equation*}
the nested commutator inside the trace expands into the $2^3=8$ operator products
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:spctr_R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S4}
	R^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1) & = (-i)\Big[\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_4
	\\
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_1^{*}
	\notag\\
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_2^{*}
	\notag\\
	                              & +\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_3
	\notag\\
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_3^{*}
	\notag\\
	                              & +\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_2
	\notag\\
	                              & +\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_1
	\notag\\
	                              & -\,\mathrm{Tr}\big\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_1)\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{I}}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\big\}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_4^{*}\Big].
	\label{eq:spctr_R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S4star}
\end{align}
Along the same lines as in linear response, the terms labelled $R_\alpha^{*}$ are the complex conjugates of $R_\alpha$ for Hermitian $\hat{\mu}$ and a real equilibrium state.
Operationally, the starred pathways $R_\alpha^{*}$ are the mirror bra/ket counterparts of the unstarred pathways $R_\alpha$: each interaction that acts on the ket in $R_\alpha$ acts instead on the bra in $R_\alpha^{*}$, and vice versa.
Phase matching in noncollinear geometries or phase cycling in collinear geometries (see~\autoref{sec:spctr_three_pulse_geometry,sec:spctr_phase_cycling}) determines which combinations $R_\alpha - R_\alpha^{*}$ contribute to the experimentally isolated rephasing and nonrephasing signals.
In typical 2DES calculations (RWA) one therefore groups the response as
\begin{equation}
	R^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)
	= (-i) \sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}\Big[ R_{\alpha}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1) - R_{\alpha}^{*}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1) \Big].
	\label{eq:spctr_S3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S1_S4star}
\end{equation}

The four pathway classes from~\autoref{eq:spctr_S3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S1_S4star} are shown in~\autoref{fig:spctr_dsfd_R1_R4}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/spctr_third_response_feynman_diagrams.pdf}
	\caption{Double-sided Feynman diagrams for the four third-order pathway classes $R_1$--$R_4$ defined in~\crefrange{eq:spctr_R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S4}{eq:spctr_R3_commutator_expansion_R1R4_S4star}.
	From left to right: $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$, and $R_4$.
	Each diagonal arrow indicates a field interaction acting on the ket (left branch) or bra (right branch) of the density matrix.
	The complex-conjugate pathways $R_\alpha^{*}$ are obtained by exchanging ket and bra actions as in~\autoref{fig:spctr_linear_response_diagrams}.}
	\label{fig:spctr_dsfd_R1_R4}
\end{figure}
For interpretation, the eight third-order pathways may be grouped into physical classes according to the density-matrix state during the waiting time $\tau_2$. %\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy}
If the system remains in the ground-state population $\ket{g}\!\bra{g}$ throughout $\tau_2$, the contribution is termed \textbf{ground-state bleach (GSB)}: depletion of $\ket{g}$ reduces the probability for subsequent ground-state absorption, and the third interaction therefore probes a diminished linear response.
If, instead, the system occupies the excited-state population $\ket{e}\!\bra{e}$ during $\tau_2$, the contribution is \textbf{stimulated emission (SE)}: the third interaction induces emission back to $\ket{g}$ and the resulting polarisation radiates coherently into the signal direction.
For the strict two-level model considered in this section, there is no higher-lying manifold to support excited-state absorption, so GSB and SE fully specify the third-order signal.

\paragraph{Phase factors of the individual pathways.}
Reading off the coherence Green function (cf.~\autoref{sec:spctr_two_level_third_order_pathways}) for each Feynman diagram yields
\begin{align}
	    \exp\bigl[-\mathrm{i}\omega_{eg}\,& f_n(\tau_3,\tau_1)\bigr],\notag\\
	    R_1 \text{\&} R_4:\quad & f_{1/4}(\tau_3,\tau_1) = \tau_3 + \tau_1,\notag\\
    R_2 \text{\&} R_3:\quad & f_{2/3}(\tau_3,\tau_1) = \tau_3 - \tau_1,
    \label{eq:spctr_pathway_phase_functions}
\end{align}
Populations during the second period $\tau_2$ carry no rapid oscillation.

For the two-level system, the third-order response $R^{(3)}$ thus contains contributions from GSB and SE only.

The extension from this pedagogical two-level limit to the multilevel manifold picture used in simulations (\,$\ket{g}$, $\{\ket{n}\}$, $\{\ket{f}\}$\,) is introduced in the model chapter (see~\autoref{chapter:model_systems}). There, an additional excited state absorption channel is defined.

In practice, $R^{(3)}$ is computed by summing the available pathways---contributions automatically generated by the Liouvillian propagator.
Having established the third-order response function $R^{(3)}(\tau_3,\tau_2,\tau_1)$ in~\autoref{sec:spctr_third_order_response} as the material kernel encoding the microscopic dynamics, the experimental realisation is now specified: a sequence of three controllable laser pulses.
This enables the isolation of different subsets of pathways via phase matching or phase cycling, in particular separating rephasing and nonrephasing signals.

% 4. Three-Pulse Experiments and Phase Matching
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Three-Pulse Experiments and Phase Matching}
\label{sec:spctr_three_pulse_geometry}
In a three-pulse experiment, the measured third-order signal is obtained by driving the system with a sequence of fields and reading out the radiated polarisation.
For finite-duration pulses with partial overlap, the separation into strictly time-ordered interactions is approximate and the signal becomes a convolution of the material response with the pulse envelopes, which modifies pathway weights and phases~\cite{doetal2017simplifiedexpressionsthat}.
Thus, the clean rephasing/nonrephasing separation discussed below should be read as the impulsive-limit idealization. In practice, finite pulse widths can mix additional effective orderings, and finite pulse bandwidth can suppress or distort pathways associated with transitions outside the laser spectrum.
In this thesis these effects are captured directly by the nonperturbative simulations.

Formally, the total field is decomposed as $E(t)=E_1(t)+E_2(t)+E_3(t)$, detailed in~\autoref{subsec:spctr_electric_fields} below.
Inserting this three-pulse decomposition into~\autoref{eq:spctr_P3_convolution_thesis} generates many terms, and the experimentally relevant contribution (e.g.\ rephasing) is selected by phase matching or phase cycling.

To understand how the field creates spatial structure in the induced polarisation, recall that the macroscopic third-order polarisation depends on space and time,
\begin{equation*}
  P^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t),
\end{equation*}
At the microscopic level, the only source of spatial dependence is the driving electric field. The molecules in the sample respond independently and do not interact with each other across macroscopic length scales. Any interactions between chromophores that are close enough to influence each other are treated as part of a single ``basic system'' (e.g.~a dimer, a pigment--protein complex, a photosynthetic antenna). Different basic systems are then driven by the same external field.

Because the field propagates as a superposition of plane waves, the only way two molecules at positions $\boldsymbol{r}$ and $\boldsymbol{r}'$ can feel a different driving is through the phase of the field,
\begin{equation*}
 \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t) \propto \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega t - \boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r})},
\end{equation*}
which depends on position. Apart from this phase, all molecules see the same field envelope, and their internal dynamics follow the same Liouville pathways. The macroscopic signal field $E_{\mathrm{S}}(\boldsymbol{r},t)$ is proportional to the third-order polarisation integrated along the propagation direction through the sample. These phase relationships are essential to understanding phase matching and phase cycling, which are the subject of the following subsections.

\subsection{Electric Fields}
\label{subsec:spctr_electric_fields}
The applied field is written as a sum of three pulses with carrier wavevectors $\boldsymbol{k}_1,\boldsymbol{k}_2,\boldsymbol{k}_3$ and carrier frequency $\omega_L$,
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	=
	\sum_{j=1}^{3}
	\Big[
	\boldsymbol{E}_j(t-t_j)\,e^{\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{k}_j\cdot\boldsymbol{r}-\omega_{L} t + \phi_j)}
	+ \text{c.c.}
	\Big],
	\label{eq:spctr_E_three_pulses_space}
\end{equation}
where $t_j$ denotes the arrival time of pulse $j$, $\phi_j$ its carrier phase, and $\boldsymbol{E}_j(t)$ a complex envelope.
In the rotating-wave approximation, the field is decomposed into positive- and negative-frequency parts,
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r},t) = \boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}(\boldsymbol{r},t) + \boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}(\boldsymbol{r},t),\qquad
    \boldsymbol{E}^{(-)} = \bigl[\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}\bigr]^*,
    \label{eq:spctr_E_plusminus}
\end{equation}
with analytic part
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	=
	\sum_{j=1}^{3}
	\boldsymbol{E}_j(t-t_j)\,
	e^{\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{k}_j\cdot\boldsymbol{r}-\omega_{L} t + \phi_j)}.
\end{equation*}
Under the RWA, only the near-resonant cross terms $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(+)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(-)}\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ are retained~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical,boyd2008chapter6nonlinear}.
Consequently, contributions to the nonlinear polarisation acquire phase factors $\pm\boldsymbol{k}_j$ and $\pm\phi_j$.
These are exploited for pathway selection by phase matching and phase cycling (see~\autoref{sec:spctr_phase_cycling}).
An optional Bloch-sphere intuition for carrier-phase kicks and pulse area is provided in~\autoref{app:phase_kick_intuition}. The main text first introduces the standard noncollinear phase-matching picture and then its collinear phase-cycling analogy used in the simulations.

\subsection{Wavevector Phase-Matching Condition}
\label{subsec:spctr_phase_matching_condition}
In nonlinear wave mixing, the signal is emitted into directions that satisfy energy and momentum conservation, $\Delta\boldsymbol{k}\approx 0$.
In a plane-wave picture, momentum conservation implies that the signal wavevector $\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{S}}$ is a signed sum of the input wavevectors,
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{S}} = \pm\boldsymbol{k}_1 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_2 \pm \boldsymbol{k}_3.
\end{equation*}
This is the \emph{phase-matching condition}.
The signs track whether the interaction involves $\boldsymbol{E}^{(+)}$ or $\boldsymbol{E}^{(-)}$ (see~\autoref{eq:spctr_E_plusminus}).
Equivalently, in Liouville space they track whether the interaction acts on the ket or bra branch of the density matrix: absorption-like interactions contribute $+\boldsymbol{k}_j$, while emission-like/complex-conjugate interactions contribute $-\boldsymbol{k}_j$.

Different sign choices correspond to different phase-matched signal directions and hence to different subsets of Liouville-space pathways.
In noncollinear geometries \emph{BOXCARS} these directions can be spatially separated.

Here, three pulses impinge on the sample from three corners of a square and meet at a point inside the sample. When viewed from above (so the four corners lie in the plane of the page and the three incoming $\boldsymbol{k}_j$ point into the sample perpendicular to the page), the projections of the wavevectors form a square. 

Drawing the three wavevectors as pointing outward from the interaction point, the fourth (bottom-right) vertex defines the phase-matched signal direction $\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{S}}$. The experimental geometry is shown in~\autoref{fig:spctr_fwm_box_car_setup}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/spctr_fwm_boxcar_schematic.pdf}
	\caption{Noncollinear BOXCARS geometry. Panel (a) shows three input beams in directions $\boldsymbol{k}_j, j = 1,2,3$, and the signal direction $\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{S}}$. Panel (b) shows the $k_x$--$k_y$ plane wavevector  projection. A local-oscillator (LO) used for heterodyne detection (see~\autoref{sec:spctr_detection_schemes}) is mixed with the signal.}
	\label{fig:spctr_fwm_box_car_setup}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Rephasing and Nonrephasing Phase-Matching Conditions}
\label{subsec:spctr_rephasing_nonrephasing}
Inserting the three-pulse field~\autoref{eq:spctr_E_three_pulses_space} into the third-order convolution~\autoref{eq:spctr_P3_convolution_thesis} and allowing only RWA-resonant terms, the third-order polarisation decomposes into components labelled by a sign triple $(l,m,n)$ with $l,m,n\in\{-1,+1\}$,
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{r},t)
	=
	\sum_{l,m,n}
	e^{\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{S}}\cdot\boldsymbol{r} - \omega_{L} t + \Phi_{lmn})}\,
	\boldsymbol{P}^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t),
	\qquad
	\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{S}} = l\boldsymbol{k}_1+m\boldsymbol{k}_2+n\boldsymbol{k}_3,\quad
	\Phi_{lmn} = l\phi_1+m\phi_2+n\phi_3.
\end{equation*}
The triple $(l,m,n)$ labels the sign choices for the three interactions and, as discussed in~\autoref{subsec:spctr_phase_matching_condition}, thereby fixes the corresponding ket/bra assignment.
Only combinations satisfying the macroscopic phase-matching condition contribute efficiently in an extended sample~\cite{boyd2008chapter6nonlinear}.

The two third-order directions of interest for 2DES are
\begin{align}
	\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{R}}    & = -\boldsymbol{k}_1 + \boldsymbol{k}_2 + \boldsymbol{k}_3, \label{eq:spctr_kR_def} \\
	\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{NR}} & = +\boldsymbol{k}_1 - \boldsymbol{k}_2 + \boldsymbol{k}_3,
	\label{eq:spctr_kNR_def}
\end{align}
usually identified as \emph{rephasing} (R) and \emph{nonrephasing} (NR) directions, respectively~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, freschetal2023twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.

Besides the RWA, a useful limiting case is the impulsive limit of well separated pulses, where the interaction times are strictly ordered and pulse overlap can be neglected~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy,mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical}.
Then the sign patterns in~\crefrange{eq:spctr_kR_def}{eq:spctr_kNR_def} can be read directly as a sequence of ket- and bra-branch interactions (see~\autoref{app:sec:phase_kick_pulse_area_intuition}).

For perfectly ordered interactions, pulses 1 and 3 determine whether the coherence phase evolves with opposite signs or with the same sign during $\tau_1$ and $\tau_3$.
Opposite signs produce the rephasing contribution, whereas equal signs produce the nonrephasing contribution.

For the rephasing signal, the sign reversal acts like a photon echo and refocuses static disorder in an inhomogeneously broadened ensemble~\cite{jonas2003twodimensionalfemtosecondspectroscopy, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, freschetal2023twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.
The nonrephasing signal does not refocus static disorder. However it gives a free-induction--decay response and is therefore directly sensitive to homogeneous linewidths~\cite{jonas2003twodimensionalfemtosecondspectroscopy}.

In the two-level example, one finds from~\autoref{eq:spctr_pathway_phase_functions} that $R_2$ and $R_3$ carry $\exp[-\mathrm{i}\omega_{eg}(\tau_3-\tau_1)]$ (rephasing), whereas $R_1$ and $R_4$ carry $\exp[-\mathrm{i}\omega_{eg}(\tau_3+\tau_1)]$ (nonrephasing).

The noncollinear discussion above establishes which pathway classes correspond to the rephasing and nonrephasing signals. In the collinear simulations used in this thesis, the same classes are selected not by spatial separation in wavevector space but by Fourier projection with respect to the carrier phases, as described next.

As a note, other third-order pathways like \textit{double-quantum coherence} can be accessed by the phase matching condition $\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{S}}=+\boldsymbol{k}_1+\boldsymbol{k}_2-\boldsymbol{k}_3$.
They are not required for the aims of this thesis.
See Ref.~\cite{caietal2024extractingdoublequantumcoherence} for a broader overview.

% 5. Phase Cycling in Collinear Geometry
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Phase Cycling and Selection of Pathways}
\label{sec:spctr_phase_cycling}
In a collinear geometry, different signal wavevector components $\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm S}$ cannot be spatially separated.%
\footnote{In numerical simulations this limitation is even more direct: spatial propagation and beam geometry are typically not modelled, so $\boldsymbol{k}$-selection cannot be implemented at the level of the fields.}
Instead, pathway selection is achieved by \emph{phase cycling}: one repeats the measurement for different carrier-phase settings $\phi_j$ and isolates the desired contribution by Fourier projection with respect to $(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)$~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, greenetal2024vibrationalcoherenceshalfbroadband}.
This works because each Liouville-space pathway acquires a characteristic phase factor determined by the sequence of light--matter interactions.

The pulse sequence and delay definitions used throughout this work are shown in~\autoref{fig:spctr_fwm_phase_cycling_setup}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/spctr_fwm_collinear_schematic.pdf}
	\caption{Collinear three-pulse excitation sequence used for phase cycling. Two pump pulses and a probe pulse interact with the sample and generate a signal field. The inter-pulse delays are labelled $\tau_1$, $\tau_2$, and $\tau_3$. All other elements, in particular the emitted signal field and the local-oscillator readout used for heterodyne detection, are as in \autoref{fig:spctr_fwm_box_car_setup}.}
	\label{fig:spctr_fwm_phase_cycling_setup}
\end{figure}

The third-order polarisation is $2\pi$-periodic in each $\phi_j$ and can be expanded as a Fourier series,
\begin{equation*}
	P^{(3)}(t,\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)
	=
	\sum_{l,m,n} e^{\mathrm{i}(l\phi_1+m\phi_2+n\phi_3)}\,P^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t),
\end{equation*}
where $P^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t)$ collects all third-order contributions carrying the phase combination $(l,m,n)$.
In particular, the rephasing and nonrephasing components correspond to
\begin{align*}
	\text{rephasing:}\quad (l,m,n)    & = (-1,+1,+1),
	                        \\
	\text{nonrephasing:}\quad (l,m,n) & = (+1,-1,+1).
\end{align*}
These index triples are the phase analogues of the wavevector combinations~\cref{eq:spctr_kR_def,eq:spctr_kNR_def}~\cite{mukamel1995principlesnonlinearoptical, cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy, greenetal2024vibrationalcoherenceshalfbroadband}.

The desired component is isolated by inverse Fourier transform with respect to the carrier phases,
\begin{equation}
	P^{(3)}_{l,m,n}(t)
	=
	\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}
	\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}
	\mathrm{d}\phi_1\,\mathrm{d}\phi_2\,\mathrm{d}\phi_3\,
	e^{-\mathrm{i}(l\phi_1+m\phi_2+n\phi_3)}\,
	P^{(3)}(t,\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3).
	\label{eq:spctr_phase_cycling_projection}
\end{equation}
In the numerical implementation, this continuous projection is replaced by a discrete Fourier sum on a finite carrier-phase grid; specifically, $\phi_1,\phi_2 \in \{0,\pi/2,\pi,3\pi/2\}$ while $\phi_3=0$, as detailed in~\autoref{app:numerical_implementation}.
Once the desired rephasing and nonrephasing components have been isolated in this way, the remaining steps are heterodyne detection and double Fourier transformation into the 2D spectrum.

\section{Construction of Two-Dimensional Spectra}
\label{sec:spctr_2D_construction_formal}
In a three-pulse experiment it is customary to parameterise the signal by the three time delays defined in~\autoref{eq:app_change_of_variables} and used in the third-order convolution~\autoref{eq:app_Pn_convolution_scalar}.
The quantities are referred to as coherence time, waiting time, and detection time. Literature standard definitions are $\tau$, $T$, and $t$ for these variables, but here unambiguous symbols $t_{\mathrm{coh}}$, $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$, and $t_{\mathrm{det}}$ are used.

The construction now proceeds from heterodyne detection of the phase-cycled time-domain signal $E(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}})$ to Fourier transformation into $\tilde{E}(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})$.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 6. Construction of Two-Dimensional Spectra
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Detection: From Polarisation to Signal Field}
\label{sec:spctr_detection_schemes}
Experimentally, the outgoing field is spectrally dispersed and recorded versus detection frequency.
Because nonlinear signals are usually weak, direct intensity detection is often inefficient: homodyne detection measures $|E_{\mathrm{S}}|^2$, which is very small when the signal field $E_{\mathrm{S}}$ is small and also discards its phase.
For 2DES, one therefore uses heterodyne detection, in which the weak signal field is mixed with a strong local oscillator (LO) field $E_{\mathrm{LO}}$, typically a phase-coherent reference derived from the same laser source.
The measured intensity is then
\begin{equation*}
	I_{\mathrm{det}}
	\propto
	\left|E_{\mathrm{LO}}+E_{\mathrm{S}}\right|^2
	= |E_{\mathrm{LO}}|^2 + |E_{\mathrm{S}}|^2
	+ 2\,\Re\!\left[E_{\mathrm{LO}}^{*}E_{\mathrm{S}}\right].
\end{equation*}
If $|E_{\mathrm{S}}| \ll |E_{\mathrm{LO}}|$, the $|E_{\mathrm{S}}|^2$ term can be neglected and the large LO background is known. After subtracting that background, the heterodyne signal becomes
\begin{equation*}
	I_{\mathrm{het}}
	\propto
	2\,\Re\!\left[E_{\mathrm{LO}}^{*}E_{\mathrm{S}}\right]
	\propto
	2\,\Re\!\left[E_{\mathrm{LO}}^{*}P^{(3)}\right].
\end{equation*}
The key point is that heterodyne detection makes the measured signal linear in $E_{\mathrm{S}}$, and therefore linear in the third-order polarisation $P^{(3)}$, which greatly improves sensitivity.

At fixed $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{det}}$, the detector still records a real heterodyne trace. Reconstruction of the complex analytic signal and global phasing are required before rephasing and nonrephasing branches can be combined into an absorptive 2D spectrum. Only after both branch signals have been reconstructed as complex quantities and placed on a common global phase may $\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{R}}$ and $\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{NR}}$ be added meaningfully.
Experimentally, the complex signal is reconstructed (e.g.\ by spectral interferometry) and globally phased~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy,hybletal1998twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.
Operationally, heterodyne detection determines the signal only up to a global phase set by the local oscillator and the phasing procedure.
If this phase is inconsistent, absorptive and dispersive components are mixed and line shapes are distorted.
Accordingly, the absorptive combination of rephasing and nonrephasing spectra in~\autoref{eq:Sab_def} assumes a consistent phasing and Fourier-sign convention for both branches.

\subsection{Double Fourier Transform and 2D Spectra}
At each fixed waiting time $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$, the phase-cycled signal comprises rephasing and nonrephasing components,
\begin{equation}
	E_{\mathrm{R/NR}}(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}}),
\label{eq:time_domain_signal}
\end{equation}
which form the time-domain input to Fourier transformation.
In some presentations, the nonrephasing branch is represented by negative $t_{\mathrm{coh}}$ values, corresponding to reversing the order of pulses 1 and 2.
Here, rephasing and nonrephasing are instead treated as distinct phase-selected components (cf.~\autoref{subsec:spctr_rephasing_nonrephasing})~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy,ginsbergetal2009twodimensionalelectronicspectroscopy}.
Using these components, the 2D spectrum is obtained by Fourier transformation with respect to $(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{det}})$.
At fixed $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$, the two-dimensional spectrum is defined as the double Fourier transform of the time-domain signal with respect to $(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{det}})$.
The opposite sign in the $t_{\mathrm{coh}}$ exponent distinguishes rephasing (photon-echo) and nonrephasing branches~\cite{cho2009twodimensionalopticalspectroscopy,greenetal2024vibrationalcoherenceshalfbroadband}.

In the numerical implementation, slowly varying complex envelopes are used. The optical carrier $e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{L} t}$ is removed. Therefore, $\omega_{\mathrm{coh}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{det}}$ are Fourier variables of these envelopes.
The 2D spectra are obtained by double Fourier transformation. For the rephasing component,
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{R}}(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})
	=
	\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\mathrm{det}}
	\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\mathrm{coh}}\,
	e^{+\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{coh}} t_{\mathrm{coh}}}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{det}} t_{\mathrm{det}}}\,
	E_{\mathrm{R}}(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}}),
	\label{eq:SR_def}
\end{equation}
and for the nonrephasing component,
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{NR}}(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})
	=
	\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\mathrm{det}}
	\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}t_{\mathrm{coh}}\,
	e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{coh}} t_{\mathrm{coh}}}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{det}} t_{\mathrm{det}}}\,
	E_{\mathrm{NR}}(t_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},t_{\mathrm{det}}).
	\label{eq:SNR_def}
\end{equation}
The waiting time is left in the time domain because it tracks the slower evolution of populations and longer-lived coherences between preparation and readout.
At short waiting times $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$, diagonal features typically dominate, while at longer $t_{\mathrm{wait}}$ off-diagonal cross peaks reveal population transfer and coupling-induced redistribution between excited states.

The absorptive 2D spectrum is obtained from the real part of the sum
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})
	=
	\Re\Big\{ \tilde{E}_{\mathrm{R}}(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}})
	+
	\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{NR}}(\omega_{\mathrm{coh}},t_{\mathrm{wait}},\omega_{\mathrm{det}}) \Big\}.
\label{eq:Sab_def}
\end{equation}
This combination removes dispersive distortions that appear when considering $|\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{R}}|$ or $|\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{NR}}|$ alone and yields peak shapes directly comparable with experimental absorptive 2D spectra~\cite{jonas2003twodimensionalfemtosecondspectroscopy,fullerogilvie2015experimentalimplementationstwodimensional}.
