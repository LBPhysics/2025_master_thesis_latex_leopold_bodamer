\section{Results}

\subsection{Benchmark validation on the monomer reference system}
\label{sec:results_monomer_benchmark}

Before analyzing coupled multilevel systems, the complete numerical workflow was
validated on the monomer benchmark introduced in~\autoref{sec:model_tls}.
This benchmark is not merely a simple preliminary example.
Rather, it tests the entire simulation chain used throughout this thesis:
explicit propagation under the time-dependent light--matter Hamiltonian,
extraction of the desired nonlinear signal by phase cycling, optional averaging
over static disorder, and the final Fourier transformation from the time to the
frequency domain.
Establishing this reference case is therefore essential before turning to dimer
spectra, where coherent beating, population transfer, and excited-state
absorption are superimposed.

The original nonperturbative 2D spectroscopy literature follows the same logic:
the two-level monomer serves as the first validation case of the
signal-extraction scheme, while the excitonic dimer is introduced only
afterwards as the minimal coupled system.
In the present work, the monomer therefore plays the same role.
It provides the benchmark against which the correctness and limitations of the
implementation can be assessed before more complex dynamics are interpreted.

\subsubsection{Benchmark logic and workflow}

All monomer calculations shown below were generated with the same general
workflow that is later used for the dimer calculations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item definition of the field-free Hamiltonian and dipole operator,
    \item propagation of the density matrix under the explicit three-pulse field,
    \item calculation of the total polarization,
    \item extraction of the desired phase-matched signal component from the
    phase-cycled runs,
    \item optional averaging over static disorder realizations,
    \item Fourier transformation with respect to coherence and detection time.
\end{enumerate}

Agreement in the final 2D spectrum alone would not guarantee that every
intermediate step is implemented correctly.
By contrast, reproducing the expected signatures both in the time domain and in
the frequency domain provides a stronger internal consistency check.

\subsubsection{Time-domain photon echo: homogeneous versus inhomogeneous ensemble}

The first monomer benchmark concerns the delayed photon-echo signal in the time
domain.
For a homogeneous ensemble, all molecules have the same transition frequency, so
the coherence created during the first time interval does not undergo ensemble
dephasing.
Consequently, no delayed rephasing signal is expected around detection time
$t\approx\tau$.
By contrast, for an inhomogeneously broadened ensemble, the distribution of
transition frequencies causes dephasing during the coherence interval, and the
three-pulse sequence can rephase this spread into a delayed photon echo.
Observing exactly this contrast is therefore a basic validation of both the
phase-cycling-based signal extraction and the treatment of static disorder.

This benchmark is interpreted in exactly this way.
The homogeneous case serves as a control showing that the code does not generate
an artificial echo in the absence of disorder, while the inhomogeneous case
verifies that the ensemble averaging and signal selection recover the delayed
rephasing expected for a photon-echo experiment.
This time-domain test is especially useful because it directly exposes whether
the implementation reproduces the physical origin of the echo before any Fourier
transformation is applied.

\subsubsection{Homogeneous monomer spectra}

The frequency-domain analysis begins with the homogeneous limit.
In the absence of static disorder, the monomer 2D spectrum is expected to show
the characteristic symmetric line shape of a homogeneously broadened two-level
system.
In the short-time overlap region the spectra can still be distorted, but outside
that regime the real part should become entirely positive, while the imaginary
part retains the corresponding dispersive structure.
Because the broadening mechanism is the same along both frequency axes, no
pronounced diagonal elongation is expected.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering

    the present figure layout is intended to show the real, imaginary, and
    absolute spectra side by side for several waiting times.}
    \label{fig:results_monomer_2d_hom}
\end{figure}

The homogeneous benchmark tests the basic Fourier pipeline without the additional
complication of disorder averaging.
It therefore isolates the effects of pulse shape, phase cycling, propagation
accuracy, and Fourier conventions.
At the same time, it provides a direct view of the pulse-overlap regime:
at the shortest population time the spectra can display phase-twist distortions,
whereas at later waiting times the line shape approaches the stationary
homogeneous limit.

\subsubsection{Inhomogeneous monomer spectra}

Once static disorder is introduced, the monomer spectrum acquires the familiar
diagonal elongation.
In a simple picture, the inhomogeneous spectrum can be understood as a
superposition of homogeneous spectra shifted along the diagonal according to the
realization-dependent transition frequency.
The antidiagonal width remains governed by the homogeneous contribution, whereas
the diagonal width is increased by the static ensemble distribution.
This makes the monomer a particularly transparent test case for checking that the
implemented disorder sampling affects the spectrum in the correct direction and
with the correct qualitative geometry.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \safefallbackgraphic[0.98\linewidth]{figures/rslts_monomer_2d_inhom_fig4}{figures/rslts_monomer_2d_inhom_(fig4)}
    \caption{Inhomogeneous monomer benchmark.

    The diagonal elongation of the spectral feature provides the central visual
    benchmark for the correct treatment of static disorder.}
    \label{fig:results_monomer_2d_inhom}
\end{figure}

The inhomogeneous result is not only a benchmark of correctness but also a visual
reference for the later coupled-system analysis.
It establishes how a single transition behaves under homogeneous and
inhomogeneous broadening before cross peaks and excited-state-absorption
features complicate the interpretation.
Because the same figure layout is used at several waiting times, the monomer also
provides a clean control showing that in the static model there is no genuine
long-time spectral evolution outside the pulse-overlap region.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What is validated by the monomer benchmark}

Taken together, the monomer results establish that the present implementation
reproduces the essential signatures expected for a two-level system in
nonperturbative 2D spectroscopy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the presence or absence of a delayed time-domain photon echo depending
    on whether static disorder is present,
    \item the correct transition from symmetric homogeneous to diagonally
    elongated inhomogeneous 2D line shapes,
    \item the confinement of pronounced spectral shape changes to the
    pulse-overlap regime at short waiting times.
\end{itemize}

This is an important result in its own right.
It means that later deviations in the dimer cannot simply be attributed to a
failure of the basic signal-construction workflow, because the minimal reference
case already behaves as expected.
The monomer section therefore serves not only as a reproduction of published
reference results, but as a validation of the full workflow used throughout the
rest of the thesis.

\subsection{Additional diagnostics beyond the minimal reference presentation}

The original benchmark literature focuses on a compact set of representative
plots.
In the present work, the monomer analysis was extended beyond that minimal set in
order to validate intermediate steps of the workflow more explicitly and to make
the construction of the final spectrum more transparent.
The most immediate extension already visible in
\autoref{fig:results_monomer_2d_hom,fig:results_monomer_2d_inhom} is the
explicit inclusion of the absolute spectrum in addition to the real and imaginary
parts.
This does not replace the physical interpretation based on the signed spectra,
but it provides a useful visual summary of the overall signal magnitude and
simplifies comparison across waiting times.

Further diagnostics that were generated or prepared for the monomer benchmark,
but are not all shown in the core paper figures, include explicit rephasing and
nonrephasing spectra, direct inspection of the 2D time-domain signals before
Fourier transformation, and convergence checks with respect to the number of
static-disorder realizations.
These quantities are methodologically valuable even when they are not all kept in
the main text, because they provide internal checks on sign conventions,
phase-cycling logic, and disorder sampling.

\subsection{Internal propagation step, output spacing, and the role of the RWA}

One practical issue that became particularly clear during the monomer benchmark
is the distinction between the stored output spacing and the internal time step
used by the ODE solver.
In the rotating-wave approximation, the propagated quantities vary on the
envelope scale, so an internal time step chosen from the pulse width is usually
sufficient.
Without the RWA, however, the rapid optical carrier oscillation must also be
resolved internally.
As a result, a physically accurate lab-frame propagation generally requires a
much smaller internal step than the spacing at which the polarization is finally
written to disk.

This distinction matters in two ways.
First, the dynamics can be propagated accurately even when the saved output grid
is comparatively coarse, provided the solver itself uses sufficiently small
internal steps.
Second, a coarse saved output spacing can nevertheless be too large for a direct
FFT of the raw lab-frame polarization, in which case aliasing may appear even
though the underlying propagation was accurate.
The monomer benchmark is well suited for making this distinction visible because
the expected spectral structure is known in advance.

\subsection{Consistency limit of Bloch--Redfield theory in the rotating frame}

A second implementation result concerns the combination of Bloch--Redfield
relaxation with a rotating-frame Hamiltonian.
In the present code, the Redfield construction evaluates transition frequencies
from the Hamiltonian used during propagation.
After transforming to the rotating frame,
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{H} \rightarrow \hat{H} - \omega_L \hat{N},
\end{equation*}
all transitions connecting states with different excitation number are shifted by
the carrier frequency.
For intramanifold couplings this shift cancels from the relevant energy
differences, but for couplings between different excitation manifolds, such as
radiative ground--excited transitions, it changes the energy gaps entering the
Redfield rates.
The resulting dissipative dynamics are then no longer based on the physical
transition energies of the original system.

This observation explains why the monomer with a Redfield construction and
\verb|rwa_sl = true| is not physically consistent in the same way as a
lab-frame calculation or a phenomenological dissipator whose rates are specified
independently of the rotated Hamiltonian.
At the same time, this limitation is itself an important result of the
benchmarking effort, because it delineates the regime in which the workflow can
be interpreted straightforwardly and the regime in which additional care is
required.

\subsection{Connection to open-system dynamics and outlook towards coupled systems}
\label{sec:2D_interpretation_OQS}

The monomer benchmark already illustrates the central link between 2D line
shapes and open-system dynamics.
Diagonal width is sensitive to static disorder, antidiagonal width reflects
homogeneous dephasing, and the short-time spectral distortions reveal the role of
pulse overlap when the system-field interaction is treated explicitly.
In this sense, even the minimal two-level model shows how coherent evolution,
decoherence, and ensemble broadening are mapped onto experimentally accessible
frequency--frequency correlations.

The temporal evolution of coherences and populations during
$(t_{\mathrm{coh}},T,t_{\mathrm{det}})$ is governed by the open-system dynamics
discussed in~\autoref{chapter:oqs_open_quantum_systems}.
In the presence of the driving field, the master equation for the reduced density
matrix takes the general form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)
    =
    \mathcal{L}_0[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)]
    -
    i\,[\hat{V}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)],
    \label{eq:ME_with_field}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_0$ is the Liouvillian of the field-free open system and
$\hat{V}(t)$ is the dipole coupling to the field.
Within the nonperturbative workflow used here, this driven equation is solved
numerically for the full pulse sequence, the polarization is extracted from the
resulting density matrix, and phase cycling together with Fourier transformation
then yields the rephasing, nonrephasing, and absorptive spectra.

The monomer benchmark therefore provides the baseline against which the later
coupled-system results must be judged.
Only once the expected behaviour of a single transition has been reproduced is it
meaningful to attribute additional features in the dimer --- such as cross peaks,
excited-state absorption, or genuine waiting-time redistribution --- to the
physics of coupling and transfer rather than to artefacts already present in the
minimal model.
